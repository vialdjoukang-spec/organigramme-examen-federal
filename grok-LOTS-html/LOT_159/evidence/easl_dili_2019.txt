Liver injury is designated hepatocellular when there is a 5-fold or higher rise in ALT alone or when the ratio of serum activity (activity is expressed as a multiple of ULN) of ALT to ALP is 5 or more. Liver injury is designated cholestatic when there is a 2-fold or higher rise in ALP alone or when the ratio of serum activity of ALT to ALP is 2 or less. When the ratio of the serum activity of ALT to ALP is between 2 and 5, liver injury is termed mixed. The pattern of DILI is determined by the first set of laboratory tests available in relation to the clinical event.

Case definitions for DILI include one of the following thresholds: ALT at least 5 times ULN, ALP at least 2 times ULN, or ALT at least 3 times ULN and total bilirubin exceeding 2 times ULN.

The CIOMS/RUCAM scale includes weighted scoring of seven domains: temporal relationship (onset and course), exclusion of alternative non-drug aetiologies, exposure to other medications, risk factors, evidence in the literature, and response to re-exposure. The total score classifies the event as highly probable (>8), probable (6-8), possible (3-5), unlikely (1-2) or excluded (≤0).

Hy's law consists of 3 components: a higher incidence of 3-fold or greater elevations of ALT or AST compared with control; individuals showing ALT or AST >3 ULN combined with serum total bilirubin >2 ULN, without initial findings of cholestasis indicated by elevated ALP; and absence of any alternative cause. In clinical practice, individual patients matching criteria 2 and 3 are referred to as Hy's law cases. Hepatocellular dysfunction is indicated by increased direct bilirubin. Bilirubin elevation may follow ALT or AST elevation with a lag of up to 4 weeks. It is anticipated to result in liver failure that is fatal or requires transplantation in at least 10 percent of cases.
