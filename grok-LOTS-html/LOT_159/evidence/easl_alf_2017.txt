ALF refers to a highly specific and rare syndrome, characterised by an acute abnormality of liver blood tests in an individual without underlying chronic liver disease. The disease process is associated with development of a coagulopathy of liver aetiology, and clinically apparent altered level of consciousness due to hepatic encephalopathy. The disturbances to coagulation required to define ALF are determined by a prolongation of INR, usually >1.5, or a prolongation of prothrombin time. In very young children and neonates, ALF may occur in the absence of encephalopathy, with a definition that requires INR >4. Exceptions without previously recognised cirrhosis: autoimmune hepatitis, Budd-Chiari syndrome, and Wilson disease.

King's College criteria, table 11. ALF due to paracetamol: arterial pH <7.3 after resuscitation and >24 h since ingestion, or lactate >3 mmol/L, or the three following criteria: hepatic encephalopathy greater than grade 3, serum creatinine >300 µmol/L, INR >6.5. ALF not due to paracetamol: INR >6.5, or 3 out of 5 following criteria: indeterminate aetiology or drug-induced hepatitis, age <10 years or >40 years, interval jaundice-encephalopathy >7 days, bilirubin >300 µmol/L, INR >3.5.

Beaujon-Paul Brousse criteria (Clichy): confusion or coma (hepatic encephalopathy stage 3 or 4), and factor V <20 percent of normal if age <30 years or factor V <30 percent if age >30 years.

Transplantation should be considered in those patients fulfilling Clichy or King's College criteria. N-acetylcysteine should be given if the patient already has coagulopathy and increased serum transaminases. A toxicology screen and paracetamol level are necessary in every patient.

Acute ischemic injury: marked elevation of aminotransferases, increased lactic dehydrogenase and creatinine, which normalise soon after stabilisation.
