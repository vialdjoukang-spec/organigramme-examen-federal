Mesenteric disease can be divided according to three characteristics: (i) presence of symptoms (or not); (ii) clinical presentation: acute, chronic, and acute on chronic ischaemia; and (iii) vessel involvement (the identification and number of involved arteries, venous obstruction, or external compression).
Chronic mesenteric ischaemia (CMI) is defined as ischaemic symptoms caused by insufficient blood supply to the gastrointestinal tract with a duration of at least 3 months.
Extrinsic compression of the mesenteric vessels can be caused by the crura of the diaphragm, or by tumour invasion, especially in pancreatic cancer.
During low flow states blood flow is redistributed to maintain perfusion of vitally important organs (brain, kidneys, and heart), at the expense of the mesenteric circulation.
Causes of aneurysmal degeneration in the mesenteric circulation include traumatic or inflammatory injury (e.g. in pancreatitis), as well as high flow in dilated collaterals.
Venous mesenteric ischaemia is usually caused by thrombosis, and consequently is usually referred to as mesenteric venous thrombosis (MVT), and these are often used as synonymous terms.
In this guideline MVT is referred to for thrombosis of mesenteric veins, which may be associated with splenic and portal vein thrombosis.
True aneurysms of the mesenteric arteries and its branches are not common, with an estimated prevalence of 0.1–2%. 17–19 With the increasing use of abdominal imaging, the majority are asymptomatic at diagnosis.
