Ferrari S, Lippuner K, Lamy O, Meier C. 2020 recommendations for osteoporosis treatment according to fracture risk from the Swiss Association against Osteoporosis (SVGO). Swiss Med Wkly. 2020;150:w20352. Publié le 29.09.2020. Lu le 31.10.2026.
DOI 10.4414/smw.2020.20352.

In Switzerland, one woman in two and one man in five will sustain a fragility fracture after the age of 50. The number of incident fragility fractures is at least 75,000 per year; 140,000 subjects have prevalent hip or vertebral fractures and 450,000 have osteoporosis (in 2010). Less than 10% of subjects with osteoporosis and only 20% of subjects with low-trauma fractures receive anti-osteoporotic medications.

Current guidelines from the SVGO dating from 2015 recommend therapy for men and women with a vertebral or hip fracture; those with bone mineral density T-score below -2.5 at spine or hip; and those with a high 10-year probability of a major osteoporotic fracture calculated by FRAX. By high risk the authors mean at least equivalent to the 10-year risk of an individual of the same age and sex with a prevalent vertebral fracture, which in Switzerland corresponds to about 10% by age 50 years and 30% by age 72 years. Patients receiving chronic glucocorticoid therapy, anti-aromatase therapy or androgen suppression therapy are also considered at high risk.

Subjects should be considered at imminent risk (greater than 10% fracture risk within 2 years) after a recent major osteoporotic fracture. Patients with a recent major osteoporotic fracture (spine, hip, pelvis, humerus or radius) have a relative risk of a second fracture increased several fold within the first 2 years, and 40% to 60% of recurrent fractures occur within those 2 years. After 65 years of age, 25% of patients with a vertebral fracture and 15% or more of those with a hip or pelvis fracture will refracture within 2 years, whereas 10% or more of those with humerus or distal radius fractures will refracture within 2 years.

Very high risk: 10-year probability of major osteoporotic fracture according to FRAX at least 20% absolute risk above the intervention threshold at any age. This corresponds to a 10-year FRAX probability of about 50% after the age of 70. Example: a 55-year-old woman with a previous wrist fracture, body mass index 20 kg/m2, who smokes, whose mother had a hip fracture, and whose hip BMD is -2.7 T-score (FRAX probability 34%). Example: a 70-year-old woman with a previous vertebral fracture, on 5 mg/d prednisone, hip BMD -3.1 T-score (FRAX probability 46%).

High risk: previous major osteoporotic fractures more than 2 years ago and/or FRAX above the intervention threshold but less than 20% above that limit, irrespective of BMD, since more than 50% of fragility fractures occur at BMD levels at or above -2.5 T-score. Chronic glucocorticoid, aromatase inhibitor or androgen suppression therapy is high risk if BMD is below a T-score of 1.5 and/or FRAX is above the intervention threshold. The French summary of the same 2020 recommendations (Gazette médicale, 2020) writes the glucocorticoid line as T-score inférieur à -1,5.

Moderate risk: BMD below -2.5 T-score, no previous fracture, FRAX below the intervention threshold.
Low risk: osteopenia (T-score above -2.5 and below -1.0) and no other risk factors.

Treatment. Imminent or very high risk with a vertebral fracture: teriparatide for 18-24 months if no recent cancer or skeletal radiation, then an antiresorptive. Imminent or very high risk with a hip fracture: zoledronate once yearly if creatinine clearance is above 35 ml/min, or denosumab. Romosozumab for 1 year then an antiresorptive in imminent or very high risk, excluding recent cardiovascular events or high cardiovascular risk. High risk, including glucocorticoids: a potent antiresorptive; denosumab is not approved for glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis; teriparatide if a vertebral fracture or spine BMD below -3.5 T-score. Moderate risk without oestrogen: consider SERMs, or an oral bisphosphonate if bone turnover markers are above the premenopausal threshold. Repeat DXA after 2 years in treated categories. Low risk: lifestyle, vitamin D 800-1000 IU/d plus or minus calcium 500-1000 mg/d if necessary, repeat DXA after 5-10 years.

FRAX clinical risk factors listed: age, sex, body mass index, previous fracture, parental hip fracture, current smoking, alcohol more than 3 units per day, glucocorticoids, rheumatoid arthritis, secondary osteoporosis (type 1 diabetes, malabsorption, chronic liver disease, hypogonadism, untreated hyperthyroidism, adult osteogenesis imperfecta).
