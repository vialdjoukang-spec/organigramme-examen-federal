There is no universally accepted definition of hyperkalaemia. This guideline has adopted the European Resuscitation Council Guideline definition with a threshold serum potassium level of ≥ 5.5 mmol/l, established in 2005 and maintained to current date.

Hyperkalaemia may be mild (K 5.5 – 5.9 mmol/l), moderate (K 6.0 – 6.4 mmol/l) or severe (K ≥ 6.5 mmol/l).

Severe hyperkalaemia requires urgent hospital assessment as it is potentially life threatening.

The most common cause of hyperkalaemia is impaired kidney function.

The treatment in hospital follows a logical 5-step approach.

Step 1 protects the heart with intravenous calcium when ECG changes are present. The text gives 30 ml of 10% calcium gluconate over 10 minutes, or 10 ml of 10% calcium chloride over 5 minutes. Calcium chloride is preferred in cardiac arrest or peri-arrest. Calcium gluconate is used for other patients with ECG signs.

Step 2 shifts potassium into cells. The text recommends insulin with glucose for severe hyperkalaemia (K ≥ 6.5 mmol/l) and suggests it for moderate hyperkalaemia (K 6.0 – 6.4 mmol/l). The numeric insulin and glucose doses of this step are not copied into the lesson, because they were not read in a Swiss professional information.

Nebulised salbutamol is recommended as adjuvant therapy for severe hyperkalaemia. The milligram dose written in this guideline is not copied into the lesson.

Serum potassium is monitored after treatment because of possible rebound.

Blood glucose is monitored after insulin-glucose.

The most commonly recognised ECG sign is peaked T waves, but on its own it is rarely a sign of life-threatening hyperkalaemia.

ECG changes may progress, but they do not always follow a fixed order.

A lithium heparin sample is preferred when a rapid urea and electrolyte result is needed.

In emergencies, potassium may be measured on a blood gas analyser while awaiting the laboratory.

Pseudo-hyperkalaemia is explored with paired samples when it is suspected.

Interventions to lower serum potassium are instituted from 5.5 mmol/l.

Most mild or moderate cases detected in the community can be managed without admission unless the patient is acutely unwell.
