Hyponatraemia, defined as a serum sodium concentration <135 mmol/l, is the most common disorder of body fluid and electrolyte balance encountered in clinical practice.

It occurs in up to 30% of hospitalised patients.

We define mild hyponatraemia as a biochemical finding of a serum sodium concentration between 130 and 135 mmol/l as measured by ion-specific electrode.

We define moderate hyponatraemia as a biochemical finding of a serum sodium concentration between 125 and 129 mmol/l as measured by ion-specific electrode.

We define profound hyponatraemia as a biochemical finding of a serum sodium concentration <125 mmol/l as measured by ion-specific electrode.

We define acute hyponatraemia as hyponatraemia documented to exist <48 h.

We define chronic hyponatraemia as hyponatraemia documented to exist for at least 48 h.

If hyponatraemia cannot be classified, we consider it chronic, unless there is clinical or anamnestic evidence of the contrary.

Severe symptoms in the symptom table include vomiting, cardiorespiratory distress, abnormal and deep somnolence, seizures and coma with Glasgow Coma Scale ≤8.

Moderately severe symptoms in the same table include nausea without vomiting, confusion and headache.

The group replaced the biochemical word severe by profound, to avoid confusion with severe symptoms.

A measured serum osmolality <275 mOsm/kg always indicates hypotonic hyponatraemia.

If urine osmolality is ≤100 mOsm/kg, accept relative excess water intake as a cause of the hypotonic hyponatraemia.

If urine osmolality is >100 mOsm/kg, interpret the urine sodium concentration.

If urine sodium concentration is ≤30 mmol/l, the text suggests accepting low effective arterial volume as a cause.

Prompt intravenous infusion of 150 ml 3% hypertonic saline over 20 min is recommended for severely symptomatic hyponatraemia.

Check the serum sodium after 20 min while repeating an infusion of 150 ml 3% hypertonic saline for the next 20 min.

Repeat until a target of 5 mmol/l increase in serum sodium concentration is achieved.

Limit the increase in serum sodium concentration to a total of 10 mmol/l during the first 24 h and an additional 8 mmol/l during every 24 h thereafter until the serum sodium concentration reaches 130 mmol/l.

Stop the hypertonic infusion when symptoms improve after a 5 mmol/l increase.

If symptoms do not improve after a 10 mmol/l increase, they are likely caused by something other than hyponatraemia.

The text uses SIAD as the overarching term because management principles are the same.

Clinical assessment of volume status is generally not very accurate.

With severe or moderately severe symptoms, the risk of brain oedema outweighs the risk of osmotic demyelination and justifies urgent treatment, irrespective of biochemical degree or of acute versus chronic timing.

In the absence of severe or moderately severe symptoms, there is time for diagnostic assessment and cause-specific treatment.

Patients are probably never truly asymptomatic. Very limited signs such as mild concentration deficits are seen even with mild hyponatraemia.

The guideline does not cover children.

Approximately 20–30% of patients with chronic heart failure in advanced classes have hyponatraemia.

The term syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis covers increased vasopressin activity and also situations without vasopressin.
