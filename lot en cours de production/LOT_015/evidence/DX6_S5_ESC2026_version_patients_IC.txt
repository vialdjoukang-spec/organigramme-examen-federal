What are Clinical Practice Guidelines?
Clinical Practice Guidelines are documents created by healthcare professionals, scientists and
patients. They are primarily intended for healthcare professionals and offer recommendations
based on the best and most recent evidence. Guidelines help ensure that patients receive the
best possible care.




What Will This Document Tell Me?
This guide for patients aims to provide you with an overview of the latest evidence-based
recommendations for the diagnosis and treatment of your condition. In particular, it should
help you to understand:

• The main types of heart failure

• The importance of looking after yourself and managing your condition

• Medicines you may be offered

• The importance of being treated by a multidisciplinary team

• Devices that might be considered appropriate

• The importance of rehabilitation

Readers interested in further details of the recommendations are kindly referred to the
ESC Clinical Practice Guidelines.

People seeking more general information about heart failure should visit
www.heartfailurematters.org

If you are a healthcare professional, the ESC hopes that this document, translated into the
language of your patients, will provide them and their caregivers with an understanding of
their diagnosis and treatment as a patient with heart failure.

Please disseminate it widely.




How Will This Document Help Me?
This document is intended to contribute to your understanding of your condition and
give you the knowledge and confidence to be involved in shared decision-making with
your healthcare providers regarding treatment and other aspects of your health. It also
provides suggestions on ways to look after yourself, which is essential in the effective
management of heart failure.
The Heart
The heart has several working parts. There is an electrical system that sends signals to keep your heartbeat
regular. A plumbing system of arteries carries blood and oxygen to the heart. Valves act like doors that
keep blood flowing in the right direction through the heart. The heart muscle acts as a pump, pushing blood
forward when it contracts (squeezes).
With each heartbeat, the right side of the heart pumps blood to the lungs to pick up oxygen, and the left side
then pumps this oxygen-rich blood out through the aorta (the main artery) to the rest of the body.

As it is a muscle, the heart can be affected by many of the same things that affect other muscles in the body
such as reduced blood supply, infections, inflammation or certain drugs.
While the heart generally works well, there are certain areas where problems can develop and any of these
can lead to heart failure. When the heart does not pump efficiently, blood and fluid can build up in the lungs
or legs, which is described as ‘congestion’.




What is Heart Failure?
Heart failure is not a single disease but a ‘syndrome’ made up of symptoms, such as breathlessness or
fatigue, that may appear alongside signs such as swollen legs.
Chronic heart failure: this is a long-term condition. Symptoms such as breathlessness, tiredness and
congestion may be there most of the time or can come and go over months or years. With the right
treatment and self-care, many people find their symptoms improve and they can do more of the things they
enjoy. People with chronic heart failure can have times when their symptoms can suddenly get worse.
Decompensated heart failure: this is when heart failure suddenly gets worse, often over hours or days.
Symptoms become much more severe, for example, a person may be much more breathless or have a lot of
fluid build-up, and they may need urgent medical treatment.
This booklet mainly refers to chronic heart failure.
Goals of treatment
For chronic heart failure, the aims of treatment are to:
• Improve quality of life and help people live longer
• Reduce the risk of symptoms suddenly getting worse (decompensated) and the need to be admitted
  to hospital

For decompensated heart failure, the aims of treatment are to:
• Stabilise symptoms as quickly as possible, often in hospital. Once stable, ongoing treatment is
  similar for chronic heart failure




Types of Heart Failure
Heart failure happens when the heart is not able to work as well as it should. This can range from mild
problems to more severe difficulties.
There are different types of heart failure, often described by how well the main pumping chamber of the
heart (the left ventricle) squeezes with each heartbeat. This is measured as the left ventricular ejection
fraction (LVEF) − the percentage of blood pumped out of the left ventricle each time it beats. LVEF is never
100% and a wide range of values can be normal for different people. What matters most is what the result
means for you, which your heart failure team will explain.
LVEF below 50% does not always mean severe heart failure and an ejection fraction over 50% does not
always mean the heart is always working perfectly. Your healthcare team will interpret your results in the
context of your overall health.
Healthcare professionals often group heart failure into two main groups:
Heart Failure with reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF)
• Having an LVEF that is less than or equal to 50% can indicate HFrEF
• The left ventricle does not squeeze as strongly as it should so less blood is pumped out with each beat
• The heart can sometimes be enlarged and the muscle may be slightly weaker than usual
• This can lead to symptoms such as breathlessness and congestion

Heart Failure with preserved Ejection Fraction (HFpEF)
• Having an LVEF more than 50% with signs and symptoms of heart failure may indicate HFpEF
• The left ventricle still squeezes well, so the amount of blood pumped does not appear to reduce
• The heart muscle may be stiffer and the ventricle might be smaller, so the heart does not relax and fill
  with blood as well as it should
• This also raises pressure in the heart and lungs and can cause the same symptoms of breathlessness,
  tiredness and congestion




Advanced Heart Failure
Advanced heart failure is a later stage of heart failure. It means that, even when on the best
treatment, there are significant symptoms that affect and limit daily life. People with advanced heart
failure often need closer follow-up, more specialist treatments and extra support with day-to-day life
and planning for the future.
Causes of Heart Failure
Heart failure has many different causes. For example, heart failure can be caused or made worse by:
• A heart attack that has damaged some of the heart muscle
• Long-term high blood pressure
• Problems with leaky or stiff heart valves
• Fast or irregular heart rhythms
• Heart muscle disorders (cardiomyopathies) that can be inherited
• Structural heart conditions that people are born with (congenital heart disease)
• Some infections, toxins or treatments that affect the heart

Diagnosis of Heart Failure
To be diagnosed with heart failure, you must have symptoms and/or signs of heart failure as well as
abnormalities in the structure and function of the heart, seen using tests.
The types of tests and investigations you may have to diagnose your condition are shown below:

Heart failure may be due to problems with the heart arteries, the heart valves, the heart rhythm or the
heart muscle.
An echocardiogram helps to see how well your heart muscle is pumping and relaxing, and whether you
have HFrEF or HFpEF. It also checks how well the heart valves are working and gives useful information
about the heart muscle itself. Depending on the suspected underlying cause, you may need additional tests.
A magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan is sometimes used to give a more detailed picture of the heart. It
can show scarring or damage to the heart muscle and certain heart muscle conditions.
The treatments you are offered will depend on how much support your heart needs to function. The more
that is known about your heart, the better treatment options can be personalised to you, which may
improve your symptoms and/or outcomes.




Symptom Severity Classification in
Heart Failure
Following diagnosis, healthcare professionals will often classify your symptoms using the New York Heart
Association (NYHA) Functional Classification system, according to the severity of your symptoms and
how they affect your physical activity:



NYHA class                Description
Class I                   No limitation of physical activity. Ordinary physical activity does not cause
                          excessive symptoms, e.g., breathlessness, fatigue or palpitations (more noticeable or
                          ‘skipping’ heartbeats)

Class II                  Slight limitation of physical activity. Comfortable at rest, but ordinary physical activity
                          causes excessive symptoms

Class III                 Significant limitation of physical activity. Comfortable at rest, but less-than-ordinary
                          physical activity causes excessive symptoms

Class IV                  Unable to do any physical activity without discomfort. Symptoms can be present even
                          at rest. If any physical activity is done, discomfort is increased
Inherited Forms of Heart Failure
Some causes of heart failure, such as certain diseases of the heart muscle (cardiomyopathies), can be
inherited, meaning they can be passed down through families. If your team think your heart failure might
be inherited – for example, because of your age, your family history or how your heart looks on scans –
they may discuss genetic testing and heart checks for close relatives.
Genetic testing and family screening can help find relatives who might be at risk so that they can be
monitored and treated early if needed. Many people with heart failure do not have an inherited condition
and not all heart problems are passed on to children. Knowing whether the cause of heart failure is
inherited can also help your team to personalise your care, including which treatments or follow-up are
most suitable.




Treatment for Heart Failure
Care from a multidisciplinary team (healthcare professionals across different professions and specialties)
is key to meeting the three major goals of treatment for people with heart failure:
1. Living a longer life
2. Preventing hospital stays due to worsening heart failure
3. Decreasing symptoms and improving quality of life

All three goals are important and it can be valuable to discuss your priorities with your family and
healthcare team. It is normal for the priorities of treatment to change over time as your health improves
with treatment or if you are experiencing a deterioration in your symptoms.

Medications for People with HFrEF
Medicines are a key treatment for HFrEF and should be started as early as possible. In many people,
medicines are the main treatment and are carefully adjusted over time, but some people may also need a
device or other procedures. Medications are normally continued alongside any devices or other
non-medicine treatments.
The ESC Clinical Practice Guidelines recommend four different types of medicines for people with HFrEF:
• Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE-I) or angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI)
• Beta-blockers (BB)
• Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA)
• Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors

You may also be given other types of medications to control your symptoms or improve your condition. For
example, diuretics (often called ‘water pills’) help your body get rid of extra salt (sodium) and fluid which
can reduce congestion and lower the pressure within the heart.




Medications for People with HFpEF
Medicines can help people with HFpEF feel better and stay well for longer. SGLT2 inhibitors and MRAs
have been shown to help people with HFpEF reduce hospital admission, decrease symptoms and
improve quality of life. Diuretics are also prescribed to help manage congestion.
Other medications can be used to help relieve symptoms or manage other heart conditions for people
with HFpEF.
Many people with HFpEF have other heart conditions including high blood pressure, coronary artery
disease or heart rhythm problems and may also be treated with ACE-I/angiotensin II receptor blockers
(ARB), BB or other medicines.
Initiation and Dosage of Heart Failure Medications
Medicines are a key treatment for all types of heart failure and should be started as early as possible.
When diagnosed with heart failure, your team will aim to start the guideline-recommended medications
and increase their doses over a short period of time. Doing this, when it is safe and tolerated, can help
improve your symptoms and reduce your risk of hospital admissions related to heart failure and dying
from heart failure.
If you are admitted to hospital because of heart failure, your medications will be optimised during
admission, and you will have follow-up appointments in the first weeks after you go home. During these
visits, your team will review how you are getting on and adjust your medicines.




Managing Heart Failure Alongside Other Health Conditions
Many people with heart failure also have other health conditions such as diabetes, kidney disease or
chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
For people with heart failure and atrial fibrillation, anticoagulants (‘blood thinners’) reduce the risk of
stroke and digoxin is sometimes given to slow a fast heart rate.
For people who have iron deficiency, your team may recommend an iron infusion to decrease
symptoms and improve quality of life.




Devices and Surgery for Heart Failure
Devices
Some people with heart failure may benefit from a small electrical device to support the heart. These
devices use painless electrical signals to help keep the heart beating safely and regularly and in some
cases, help it pump more effectively. They are usually placed under the skin near the collarbone using
local anaesthetic.

Each person’s experience with heart failure is different. Even with the medications, devices and
surgical treatments discussed in this booklet, symptoms can get worse.
A supportive approach from all members of your multidisciplinary team can improve quality of
life by balancing medical treatment and symptom control with particular attention to mental and
spiritual wellbeing.




Advance Care Planning

Advance care planning means talking with your healthcare team and those close to you about your
values, what matters most to you and the type of care and treatments you would or would not want if
your heart failure gets worse.
These conversations can help make sure that future decisions about your care reflect your wishes as
far as possible.


     Heart failure is a long-term condition, and it can feel overwhelming, but with good treatment,
     support and self-care, many people live well for many years. We hope this booklet helps you feel
