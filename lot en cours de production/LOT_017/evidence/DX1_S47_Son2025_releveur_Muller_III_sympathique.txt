EXTRAITS (texte intégral, mot pour mot) :
Ptosis, also known as blepharoptosis, refers to drooping of the upper eyelid. It can affect both eyes or only one eye, leading to decreased vision or even complete eye occlusion ( 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ). Ptosis is classified into congenital and acquired ptosis depending on the age of onset. Acquired ptosis is classified into neurogenic, aponeurotic, myogenic, traumatic, and mechanical ptosis depending on the cause ( 4 ). In adults, the most common type of acquired ptosis is aponeurotic ptosis, mainly caused by disinsertion of the levator aponeurosis ( 5 , 6 ). 

The levator palpebrae superioris and Müller’s muscles are the two retractor muscles of the upper eyelid ( Fig. 1 ). The levator palpebrae superioris is the primary retractor muscle responsible for the elevation of the upper eyelid. It is a skeletal muscle innervated by the superior division of the oculomotor nerve. It originates just above the annulus of Zinn and is inserted into the superior tarsal plate ( 4 , 7 , 8 ). The Müller’s muscle—a smooth muscle with sympathetic innervation—arises from the underside of the levator palpebrae superioris and is inserted into the superior tarsal plate. It helps the levator palpebrae superioris to raise the upper eyelid, and is responsible for approximately 1–2 mm of the additional elevation ( 4 , 8 ). Therefore, any problems with the neural pathways that innervate these two muscles (levator palpebrae superioris and Müller’s muscle) can cause neurogenic ptosis. Fig. 1

Müller’s muscle originates from the levator palpebrae superioris, inserts onto the tarsal plate, and helps the levator palpebrae superioris to raise the upper eyelid. 

Marginal reflex distance-1. 

Berke’s method is commonly used to evaluate the function of the levator palpebrae superioris ( 14 ). The moving distance of the upper eyelid margin from downward gaze to upward gaze is measured, and it reflects the degree of levator palpebrae superioris function (≥10 mm: excellent; 6–9 mm: good; 4–5 mm: fair; <4 mm: poor) ( 14 , 15 ). 

The phenylephrine test can be used to assess the function of the Müller’s muscle. A drop of 2.5% or 10% phenylephrine is instilled in the patient’s eye under the affected upper eyelid. If MRD-1 increases within a few minutes, it is considered a positive reaction that indicates dysfunction of the Müller’s muscle ( 16 , 17 , 18 ). 

The oculomotor nerve emerges from the oculomotor nucleus in the midbrain. It carries the motor nerve fibers to some of the extraocular muscles (levator palpebrae superioris, superior rectus, medial rectus, inferior rectus, and inferior oblique muscles). Among the subnuclei, a single centrally-placed caudal nucleus innervates the levator palpebrae superioris muscles. Parasympathetic nerve fibers emerge from the Edinger–Westphal nucleus, an accessory oculomotor nerve nucleus located in the rostral portion of the oculomotor nuclear complex. It innervates the ciliary and iris sphincter muscles. As the oculomotor nerve exits the brainstem, the parasympathetic nerve fibers travel on its superficial part ( 19 , 20 , 21 ). Therefore, disruption of the oculomotor nerve can cause impairment of eyelid elevation, pupillary constriction, and accommodation ( 19 , 21 ). The oculomotor nerve pathway can be divided into four segments: intra-axial, subarachnoid, cavernous, and orbital ( Fig. 3 ). Fig. 3 

The oculomotor nerve originates from the oculomotor nucleus of the midbrain. The nucleus is located ventral to the cerebral aqueduct at the level of the superior colliculus, and is composed of multiple subnuclei. The subnucleus for the levator palpebrae superioris muscle is located at the midline of the caudal region, and the remaining subnuclei are found on both sides. The fascicles from the nuclear complex join, pass through the red nucleus, and exit ventrally ( Fig. 4 ) ( 19 , 21 ). As a lesion involving the intra-axial segment usually affects the entire nucleus, the patient may experience bilateral ptosis, adduction impairment, upward and downward gaze abnormalities, and mydriasis ( 19 , 21 , 22 ). However, unilateral ptosis may occur if the lesion involves pure fascicles. The most common pathologies involving the intra-axial segment in the midbrain are infarcts ( Fig. 5 ); hemorrhage, inflammatory or demyelinating lesion, neoplasm, and vascular malformations ( 19 , 23 ). Subsequently, the oculomotor nerve fibers enter the orbital fossa through the superior orbital fissure, and the nerve splits into superior and inferior divisions. The superior division runs above the optic nerve and innervates the levator palpebrae superioris and superior rectus muscles. The inferior division runs below the optic nerve (CN II) and innervates the medial rectus, inferior rectus, and inferior oblique muscles. Parasympathetic fibers travel along the inferior division. They then separate from the division, synapse in the ciliary ganglion, and supply the ciliary and iris sphincter muscles ( 19 , 21 , 28 ). 

The oculosympathetic pathway is long and circuitous, beginning in the hypothalamus and ending in the eyes and face ( Fig. 15 ). It consists of three types of neurons: first-order (central), second-order (preganglionic), and third-order (postganglionic). Therefore, several pathologies can involve this pathway ( 32 , 34 ). To distinguish the interruption of the third-order neuron from that of the first- or second-order neurons, 1% hydroxyamphetamine can be used. Hydroxyamphetamine releases presynaptic norepinephrine into the synaptic gap. Therefore, pupils dilate if third-order neurons are intact. However, if third-order neurons are interrupted, the pupil does not dilate because there is no norepinephrine at the terminal synapse of the sympathetic nerve ( 32 , 33 , 34 ). Fig. 15 

Pathway of the oculosympathetic nerve. The schematic diagram shows the course of the oculosympathetic nerve from the hypothalamus to the orbit. The first-order neurons (blue line) arise from the hypothalamus and descend through the brainstem and the spinal cord before synapsing in the ciliospinal center located at C8 to T2 levels. The second-order neurons (pink line) exit the spinal cord through the ventral roots and ascend through the inferior and middle cervical ganglia toward the superior cervical ganglion. The inferior and middle cervical ganglia are connected by two or more cords composed of anterior (also known as ansa subclavia) and posterior loops. The anterior loops run above the cervical pleura and loop beneath the first portion of the subclavian artery. The third-order neurons (green line) originate from the superior cervical ganglion and ascend along the internal carotid artery to form a nerve plexus. Finally, the nerve enters the orbital fossa through the superior orbital fissure, innervating the Müller’s and pupil dilator muscles. 

Post-ganglionic neurons originating from the superior cervical ganglion ascend along the internal carotid artery through the adventitia and form a nerve plexus ( 32 , 42 ). The plexus then merges, runs through the cavernous sinus, and enters the orbit through the superior orbital fissure ( Fig. 21 ). The fibers finally innervate the Müller’s muscle, which is responsible for 1–2 mm of upper lid elevation, and the pupil dilator muscle (the sympathetic fibers responsible for facial sweating are divided and run along internal and external carotid arteries to innervate the face) ( 32 , 33 , 42 ).
