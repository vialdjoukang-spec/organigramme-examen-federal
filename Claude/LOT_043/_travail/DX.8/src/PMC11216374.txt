Medical therapy of pericarditis should be targeted at the aetiology as much as possible. Unfortunately, many cases of acute and recurrent pericarditis remain without a clear aetiology and are labelled as idiopathic after diagnostic workup. For these patients, empiric anti-inflammatory therapy is currently used in clinical practice with the aim of controlling symptoms (mainly pericarditis chest pain), and preventing complications, especially the most troublesome and common represented by recurrent pericarditis.
Although pericarditis is rarely responsible for mortality, especially when recurrent, it can seriously impair the quality of life. An appropriate empiric anti-inflammatory treatment is of paramount importance to reduce the duration of the disease and prevent recurrences.
The aim of the present practical clinical review is to highlight the principles of anti-inflammatory therapies based on published evidence and guidelines, providing an expert perspective with tips and tricks for clinical practice.
Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs
NSAIDs are the mainstay of empiric anti-inflammatory therapy for pericarditis (Fig. ) in clinical practice and the first choice, unless contraindicated or in the absence of specific indications for treatment., The primary effect of NSAIDs is the inhibition of cyclooxygenase-COX (prostaglandin synthase), thereby impairing the ultimate transformation of arachidonic acid to prostaglandins, prostacyclin and thromboxane.
Fig. 1Different levels of treatment for pericarditis. The first level and option are represented by a NSAID and colchicine. Second level is represented by corticosteroids combined with colchicine. Third level is the combination of a NSAID, colchicine and corticosteroids. Fourth level is the use of anti-IL-1 agents that can be associated with colchicine. In case of failure of the fourth level, published pharmacological alternatives are discussed in the text. Pericardiectomy is the last option in the United States, but it is usually not considered in Europe in the absence of pericardial constriction.
The efficacy of NSAIDs to treat pericarditis has been tested in a single clinical trial in patients with postpericardiotomy syndrome, where ibuprofen and indomethacin were more efficacious than placebo to control symptoms and halved the recurrence rate. There are no additional clinical trials and their indication for pericarditis is essentially based on experts’ opinion, and common clinical practice (level of evidence B: evidence from a single randomized controlled trial – RCT).
In the literature, the most common reported drugs include aspirin, ibuprofen and indomethacin. In clinical practice. most common mistakes include the use of too low doses or wrong time intervals. As pericarditis is an inflammatory disease, anti-inflammatory drugs should be used at full anti-inflammatory doses (e.g. 750–1000 mg for aspirin, 600–800 mg for ibuprofen and 50 mg for indomethacin), considering the appropriate time interval of administration according to drug pharmacokinetics (Table ). A gastroprotection should be provided for all patients on NSAIDs with a full dose of a proton pump inhibitor as for peptic ulcer disease (e.g. esomeprazole 40 mg once daily, lansoprazole 30 mg once daily, and pantoprazole 40 mg once daily). Commonly used NSAIDs should be given every 8 h to achieve full control of symptoms and maintained until their resolution with normalization of inflammatory biomarkers (usually C-reactive protein), regression of ECG changes, and usually regression of pericardial effusion. In case of failure or incomplete response to one NSAID, a second alternative NSAID should be tested before resorting to an alternative class of drugs (e.g. corticosteroids).,

After clinical remission it is suggested to taper the NSAID maintaining the appropriate time interval between two doses (Table ).
Colchicine is now a well established anti-inflammatory therapy to be added on top of NSAIDs or corticosteroids. Colchicine is a lipophilic drug that freely enters cells and it is eliminated by glycoprotein-P. As neutrophils are not expressing this membrane protein, colchicine is concentrated in these cells, even when used at low oral doses, such as 0.5 mg. The drug blocks tubulin polymerization and interferes with microtubule function affecting several pro-inflammatory functions of white blood cells (chemiotaxis, degranulations, phagocytosis), including the assembly of the inflammasome, a cytosolic complex of proteins that is responsible for the generation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, the most important being IL-1.
Based on these actions, colchicine was well tolerated and efficacious for the treatment of polyserositis in the setting of Familial Mediterranean Fever (FMF), a monogenic auto-inflammatory disease characterized by an inappropriate and/or exaggerated activation of inflammation for unusual or mild triggers, such as cold exposure, fatigue or an infection.
Based on its efficacy in the setting of pleuro-pericarditis associated with FMF, colchicine has been successfully used in idiopathic recurrent pericarditis.– Its efficacy and safety have been tested and proven in several RCTs, in the setting of either acute or recurrent pericarditis.– Avoiding a loading dose and using weight-adjusted doses (0.5 mg once daily for patients <70 kg or 0.5 mg twice daily for those >70 kg), colchicine halved the recurrence rate in patients with acute and recurrent pericarditis, and it is recommended for at least 3 months for a first episode, or 6 months in case of recurrences (Table ).
Colchicine is a registered treatment for pericarditis in some European countries, such as Italy. On this basis, it is no longer an experimental drug and should be prescribed in patients with pericarditis (first episode or recurrence) in association with a NSAID, a corticosteroid, or combined with both as triple therapy in more complicated and difficult-to-treat cases. Colchicine alone is not sufficient to control the disease in the acute phase and should be combined with a second anti-inflammatory drug.
The most common reported side effect is represented by diarrhoea in up to 10% of cases, while less common side effects include: transaminases elevation (about 4%), reduction of white blood cells (up to 1%), and alopecia with prolonged use, especially reported in women.
Corticosteroids have been widely used in the past for the treatment of pericarditis and pericardial effusion for a fast effect on symptoms and inflammatory manifestations of pericarditis. First European guidelines have recommended high doses of corticosteroids (e.g. prednisone 1.0 mg/kg/day or equivalent corticosteroid). Indiscriminate use of corticosteroids, especially with high doses, and fast tapering regimens were associated with common side effects (severe in up to 25% of cases), leading to discontinuation and more recurrences., The most recent European guidelines have recommended the use of low-to-moderate doses (e.g. prednisone 0.2–0.5 mg/kg/day) as a second option after NSAIDs and colchicine. Nowadays the use of these drugs is often delayed for fear of promoting further recurrences and chronicity. However, it should be acknowledged that it is the wrong use of corticosteroids (too high doses, fast tapering before full clinical remission) that is responsible for a complicated course. Corticosteroids at low-to-moderate doses have specific indications, when they could be considered as the first option. These conditions include: contraindications or failure of NSAIDs, combination therapy with a NSAID and colchicine in more difficult cases (triple therapy), patients with specific indications for these drugs (e.g. systemic inflammatory disease, vasculitis, postpericardiotomy syndromes, postvaccination forms), interfering drugs (e.g. concomitant indication for oral anticoagulants) and specific physiological conditions, such as pregnancy after week 20, where use of aspirin and a NSAID at high doses may be detrimental, or specific comorbidities, where NSAIDs can be detrimental (typically patients with moderate-to-severe renal failure) (Fig. ).,

In most complicated cases, pericarditic chest pain may be controlled only by the combination of different anti-inflammatory therapies: a NSAID plus colchicine plus low-to-moderate doses of corticosteroids (triple therapy). Combination therapies are common practice in cardiology in different cardiovascular diseases (e.g. ischemic heart diseases, heart failure, systemic hypertension, hypercholesterolemia). In such conditions, combination therapy has several advantages: a combination of drugs at lower doses can reduce the risk of side effects and has a stronger efficacy compared with a single pharmacological agent at higher doses.
Several cases of complicated pericarditis are related to delayed diagnoses, inappropriate use of anti-inflammatory drugs for dosing, duration of therapy or lack of appropriate tapering. However, some patients with recurrent pericarditis have difficult-to-treat episodes even on triple anti-inflammatory therapy (NSAID + colchicine + corticosteroid), often leading to corticosteroid dependence, that is inability to reduce or withdraw a corticosteroid.
In such patients, especially in cases with elevation of C-reactive protein, a new class of drugs, anti-IL-1 agents, have been proven to be efficacious to control the disease.,
Anti-IL-1 agents are drugs that specifically target the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1 alfa, released by inflamed or injured pericardial cells, and IL-1 beta released by inflammatory cells. IL-1 alfa triggers the activation of IL-1 beta. There are three main agents: anakinra, rilonacept and canakinumab (Fig. ).

Rilonacept is a trap for IL-1 alfa and beta and also the natural inhibitor of IL-1. It is given every week subcutaneously. Canakinumab is a human antibody targeting IL-1 beta. Anakinra and rilonacept target both IL-1 alfa and beta and have been proven to be efficacious for pericarditis treatment. Canakinumab only targets IL-1 beta and it is less efficacious for the treatment of pericarditis. Anakinra has been the first agent used for pericarditis. Based on its safety and efficacy in children with autoinflammatory disease, it was empirically used to treat paediatric corticosteroid-dependent cases of recurrent pericarditis to allow withdrawal of corticosteroids, and to reduce severe side effects in this population. A subsequent spontaneous trial demonstrated the efficacy and safety of anakinra for patients with corticosteroid-dependent, colchicine-resistant recurrent pericarditis with elevation of C-reactive protein. These findings were also confirmed in a real-world international registry, and in a subsequent sponsored trial for rilonacept, that led to its registration for recurrent pericarditis in the United States. Currently, anakinra can be prescribed in European countries. In Italy, it has been approved for use in corticosteroid-dependent, colchicine-resistant recurrent pericarditis with a specific program under 648/1996 law that allows the off-label use of drugs of proven efficacy for a nonregistered indication. This prescription requires informed consent with a therapeutic plan to be delivered., It is also requested to monitor blood cell count, renal function, transaminases and, CK at 1, 3 and 6 months. The most common reported side effect is related to local skin reactions at the site of injection that usually occur after 1–2 weeks of therapy and can be managed with antihistaminic drugs orally and/or topically. Additional possible side effects include transaminase elevation (4%), reduction in white blood cells (1%) and a small increase in skin and respiratory infections reported to be around 4% for anakinra but higher for rilonacept.,
Anti-IL-1 agents are usually considered after failure of NSAIDs, colchicine and corticosteroids in patients with an inflammatory phenotype of presentation with fever and/or elevation of C-reactive protein, and usually pericardial effusion at each recurrence. They can be used together with colchicine with some preliminary experience suggesting a synergistic action to prevent recurrences by the combined use of both drugs. In case of response to these drugs, usually within 1 week, tapering of concomitant anti-inflammatory therapies with NSAID and corticosteroid is recommended, while maintaining colchicine as background anti-inflammatory therapy.
Azathioprine and hydroxychloroquine
There is limited evidence to support the use of alternative drugs beyond NSAIDs, colchicine, corticosteroids and anti-IL-1 agents.
Either azathioprine or hydroxychloroquine are potential cheap therapeutic options that could be used as steroid-sparing agents., Azathioprine (2 mg/kg/day) has been reported in case reports and small series on top of corticosteroids to allow reduction and tapering of these drugs.
Hydroxychloroquine (400 mg/day) has been tested in patients with corticosteroid-dependent, colchicine-resistant recurrent pericarditis in a post hoc analysis of prospectively collected data of 15 patients showing a corticosteroid-sparing effect and a prolongation of the time to the subsequent recurrence.
Human intravenous immunoglobulins are a therapeutic option that has been used in patients with refractory recurrent pericarditis after failure of previous conventional treatments, including NSAIDs, colchicine, corticosteroids and more recently also anakinra. Immunoglobulins are derived from plasma of healthy blood donors, and include human immunoglobulins that could target both infectious agents and autoantibodies, and have the potentiality to modulate the immune response, being potentially useful for either infectious or noninfectious forms of pericarditis. These drugs are used as an off-label indication and are rather expensive compared with previously mentioned therapies. The usual scheme used for recurrent pericarditis is to provide 400–500 mg/kg/day for 5 consecutive days. A cycle of treatment can be repeated after 1 month.,
The current proposed stepwise algorithm of medical therapy starts with a NSAID (aspirin or ibuprofen) plus colchicine as first choice (Fig. )., Indomethacin, another commonly used NSAID for pericarditis, is usually reserved for recurrences, after failure of aspirin and ibuprofen, as it has a stronger anti-inflammatory effect, but it is less well tolerated than ibuprofen and aspirin. In specific cases, corticosteroids may be considered as a first option for specific indications (e.g. postpericardiotomy syndrome, postvaccine pericarditis). In case of contraindications, failure or intolerance to NSAIDs, usually after at least 1 week of therapy and allowing testing of a second NSAID, corticosteroids at low-to-moderate doses should be considered with colchicine.
In more complicated cases with refractory symptoms, it is recommended to combine a NSAID with colchicine and corticosteroids (triple therapy). Tapering of the attack dose can be considered only after clinical remission. Tapering starts from NSAIDs; when NSAIDs are withdrawn, if the patient is asymptomatic and in clinical remission, tapering is extended to corticosteroids with a slow tapering. Colchicine is maintained until the end of treatment, and it is wise to maintain the dose for up to 6 months after the withdrawal of corticosteroids. In case of recurrent symptoms during tapering, it is prudent to stop tapering, trying to control symptoms by increasing or adding a NSAID, and then restarting a slower tapering after control of symptoms.
Physical rest is an important component of medical therapy. An increase in the heart rate can enhance the friction of pericardial layers and worsen symptoms. It is recommended to abstain from physical activity until complete symptom resolution and clinical remission. In patients with persistent symptoms despite optimal medical anti-inflammatory therapy and elevated heart rate, beta blockers have been administrated with improved control of heart rate and symptoms.
On the way to personalized therapy for pericarditis
The current evidence of a genetic background that may favour an autoinflammatory response with new recurrences of pericarditis for unusual triggers (e.g. cold exposure, infection, fatigue), and the publication of clinical trials and registries, providing evidence of a strong efficacy of anti-IL-1 agents, has clearly demonstrated the importance of better knowledge of the pathogenesis of pericarditis with its complex interplay between genetic background, infectious and noninfectious triggers, inflammation and autoimmunity.,, A better understanding of this pathogenesis could improve medical therapy, targeting the pathophysiology and personalizing the treatment.
At present, patients with pericarditis can be characterized by the presence or absence of systemic inflammation activation by means of fever and/or elevated C-reactive protein,, pericardial and pleural effusion at each recurrence. Patients with striking inflammatory features should be considered to have an inflammatory phenotype that could be more responsive to colchicine and anti-IL-1 agents. A minority of patients may show a noninflammatory phenotype. For these patients, alternative options should be corticosteroids together with steroid-sparing agents, and immunoglobulins after failure of other medical therapies.
Conclusions future perspectives
Ongoing research is now focused on a better understanding of the pathophysiology of pericarditis beyond the classical view of a simple aetiology based on a single infectious or noninfectious agent. Better understanding of the complex interplay between genetic predisposition, inflammation and autoimmunity will help to develop more tailored treatments in the single patient. Moreover, the imaging evidence of pericarditis by second-level imaging techniques, such as cardiac magnetic resonance, will help to provide more precise and objective evidence of disease and will help to improve diagnostic capabilities and individualize the duration of therapies.
