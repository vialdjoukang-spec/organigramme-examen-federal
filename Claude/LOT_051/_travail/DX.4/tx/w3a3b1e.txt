We aimed to assess whether Pelargonium sidoides extract (EPs®7630) reduces symptom duration or antibiotic use compared with usual care in adults with acute bronchitis. We conducted a pragmatic randomised-controlled trial across 36 primary care practices and five walk-in clinics in Switzerland. Adults ( ≥ 18 years) consulting a general practitioner for the first time for a new episode of acute bronchitis, with a cough of up to eight days’ duration, were eligible for inclusion. The co-primary outcomes were (1) number of days required to achieve a 50% reduction in symptoms from the peak value, assessed using the Acute Bronchitis Severity Score (ABSS), and (2) the proportion of participants who used antibiotics. Missing data in intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses were multiply imputed. 332 participants were enrolled and randomly assigned: 155 to EPs®7630 and 177 to usual care. Neither co-primary outcomes showed a statistically significant difference between groups. No significant difference in time to 50% reduction of symptoms between the EPs®7630 and usual care groups was observed (adjusted regression coefficient 0.05 [95% CI − 0.13–0.23]; p = 0.578). Antibiotic use was 7 percentage points lower (31% relative reduction) in the EPs®7630 group (17.4%, 20 of 155) than in the usual care group (25.2%, 33 of 177), although the difference was not statistically significant (adjusted risk ratio 0.78 [95% CI 0.49–1.26]; p = 0.309). Adverse events were reported more frequently in the EPs®7630 group (32.3%, 50 of 155) than in the usual care group (21.5%, 38 of 177; hazard ratio 1.40 [95% CI 1.03–1.89]; p = 0.030); all adverse drug events were mild. EPs®7630 did not reduce symptom duration or antibiotic use significantly and was associated with more frequent events, that were all mild and previously described. Despite the absence of statistical significance, the observed reduction in antibiotic use warrants further investigation in larger trials to clarify its potential role within antimicrobial stewardship strategies.

In Switzerland, every year, 5.5% of the population consult a general practitioner for this problem 2 . It is a transient inflammation of the trachea and main bronchi, characterised by an acute cough lasting up to three weeks 1 . Although usually self-limiting, acute bronchitis is a major cause of healthcare use and work absence 3 . Despite being predominantly viral, 41.5% of patients diagnosed with acute bronchitis in Switzerland receive antibiotics 4 . Inappropriate antibiotic prescribing for this condition contributes to the growing global burden of antimicrobial resistance 5 . Beyond antibiotics, general practitioners frequently prescribe symptomatic treatments such as decongestants, anti-inflammatories, expectorants, antihistamines, and opioid cough suppressants, despite limited evidence supporting their efficacy 6 . Some of these treatments, such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and vasoconstrictive nasal sprays, can occasionally cause serious adverse effects 7 . In Switzerland, most of these treatments are reimbursed under mandatory basic health insurance, which covers consultations as well as the majority of prescribed medications. As patients often seek medical care to receive treatment for symptom relief and general practitioners face difficulties with providing effective treatments 8 , herbal products are increasingly considered as potential supportive treatments 9 .
[w3a3b1e-3] Pelargonium sidoides (P. sidoides) , a plant native to South Africa, has been used for centuries for the treatment of coughs and tuberculosis by different ethnic groups 10 . Its root extract exhibits antibacterial, immunomodulatory, and antiviral properties 11 . A standardized alcoholic extract of P. sidoides , EPs®7630, has been formulated and commercialized in Europe since 1991, under the name of Kaloba® (Dr Willmar Schwabe GmbH & Co. KG, Karlsruhe, Germany) 12 . A Cochrane meta-analysis suggested possible benefits of this extract for the treatement of acute bronchitis 13 , but the certainty of evidence was rated as low, because of the small number of studies conducted in a few countries, possible publication bias, and industry sponsorship 14 – 17 . The most frequently reported adverse effects with EPs®7630 in the literature are gastrointestinal symptoms (such as nausea, diarrhea, and abdominal pain) and hypersensitivity reactions 18 . Less commonly reported adverse events include mild bleeding episodes such as epistaxis and gingival bleeding 19 . As the effectiveness of EPs®7630 in adults with acute bronchitis therefore remains uncertain, the authors of this meta-analysis call for independent research using rigorous methods and other clinically relevant outcomes, such as antibiotic use 13 .
[w3a3b1e-4] We conducted an independent, pragmatic, randomised controlled trial in Swiss general practices and walk-in clinics to evaluate whether P. sidoides extract (EPs®7630) reduces symptom duration or antibiotic use compared with usual care.
[w3a3b1e-5] Methods Study design and participants Phytobronch was an investigator-initiated, pragmatic, open-label, superiority randomised controlled trial. Participants were recruited from general practices and walk-in clinics located in rural as well as in urban regions of the French-speaking cantons of Switzerland. The initial plan included approximately 40 general practices and two walk-in clinics; due to slow recruitment, three additional walk-in clinics were added in October 2024, increasing the total number of participating general practitioners to 89. General practitioners were eligible if they were currently practicing in the French-speaking part of Switzerland. They were recruited via email. As remuneration, general practitioners were paid for each patient included in the trial. The trial protocol and statistical analysis plan are included in the supplementary material ( Supplementary material pp 22–78 and pp 79–93). The study was approved by the Swiss ethics committee of the canton of Vaud (2023-00125) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines.
[w3a3b1e-6] Adults (≥18 years) who presented to participating general practices or walk-in clinics with symptoms suggestive of acute bronchitis were screened for eligibility by general practitioners during the consultation, with inclusion and exclusion criteria documented in the eCRF for eligible participants. Inclusion criteria were a first consultation for an acute episode of bronchitis and cough lasting no more than eight days. Exclusion criteria included pneumonia or another infection requiring antibiotics; non-infectious causes of cough (such as allergy or asthma); immunodeficiency (including treatment with immunosuppressants or chemotherapy); high bleeding risk (including anticoagulant therapy); severe hepatic disease; pregnancy or breastfeeding; inability to provide informed consent or complete the symptom diary; known hypersensitivity to P. sidoides extract or any excipients in the investigational medication; and current use of Kaloba® or Umckaloabo® (over-the-counter formulation of EPs® 7630) for the present episode of bronchitis. All participants provided written informed consent before undergoing any study procedures.
[w3a3b1e-7] Randomisation and blinding Data collection and randomisation were managed through Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap®) hosted at the University of Fribourg. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either usual care or EPs®7630, with stratification and block randomisation by general practitioner (block size of 16). Randomisation codes were generated by an independent statistician and implemented electronically in REDCap® using its “Randomize” function. Patients were randomized after confirmation of eligibility. Randomization was performed by the general practitioner using the REDCap® eCRF “Randomize” function, which automatically displayed the allocation group. This was an open-label study; no placebo was used, and treatment allocation was not masked to general practitioners or participants.
[w3a3b1e-8] Procedures After providing written informed consent, participants’ sociodemographic and clinical data were collected by general practitioners at baseline. This included age, sex, highest level of educational attainment, categorised as lower secondary education (compulsory school), apprenticeship (vocational training), upper secondary academic or post-secondary vocational training, university level (including universities of applied sciences). Clinical data included relevant comorbidities, smoking status, concomitant medication, as well as cough symptom duration and severity of current acute bronchitis episode which was assessed using the Acute Bronchitis Severity Score (ABSS), detailed below 20 .
[w3a3b1e-9] Intervention Participants assigned to the intervention group received Pelargonium sidoides extract (Kaloba®, EPs®7630; Dr Willmar Schwabe GmbH & Co. KG, Karlsruhe, Germany), administered according to the manufacturer’s dosing recommendations. The standardised liquid extract (EPs®7630), which holds marketing authorisation for acute bronchitis in Switzerland, was selected. Participants received a 50 mL bottle of EPs®7630 in its commercial packaging, accompanied by the patient information leaflet describing known adverse drug effects. Participants were instructed to take 30 drops three times daily for seven days, continuing until symptom resolution for up to 21 days. If they finished the initial bottle (intended to last ten days), they could request a replacement from their study centre. Paracetamol could be prescribed, and regular medications continued, if not contraindicated; both were documented in REDCap® at baseline.
[w3a3b1e-10] Control group The control group received usual care for acute bronchitis provided by the general practitioner, excluding herbal medicine. Prescribed medications were categorised by the general practitioners in REDCap® as antipyretic, anti-inflammatory, antitussive, expectorant, or other.
[w3a3b1e-11] In both groups, a delayed (“back-up”) antibiotic prescription could be issued at the discretion of the general practitioner if symptoms failed to improve within the expected timeframe advised by the general practitioner. Use of over-the-counter medication was allowed, as access to such medication could not be controlled. All participants were asked to complete an online daily diary in REDCap® to record symptom evolution, treatment adherence, and all (prescribed and over-the-counter) co-medication until symptom resolution or up to 22 days after inclusion. Participants received automated daily reminders by email or SMS. All participants were also contacted by telephone at day 4, 8 and 22 to minimise missing data from incomplete daily diaries and to collect information on adverse events (AEs), severe adverse events (SAEs), treatment adherence, and antibiotic use.
[w3a3b1e-12] Outcomes The co-primary endpoints were: (1) the number of days required to achieve a 50% reduction in symptoms from peak severity, measured using the Acute Bronchitis Severity Score (ABSS), and (2) the proportion of participants who used antibiotics. The symptom peak was defined as the highest ABSS value recorded, or the initial score if this was the maximum 21 .
[w3a3b1e-13] The ABSS assesses five symptoms—overall severity of acute bronchitis, frequency of coughing during the day and at night, fever, and ability to perform daily activities—each rated on a four-point scale, for a maximum total score of 20. The ABSS was selected as a measure of severity because it relies solely on patient-reported symptoms and does not require clinical examination, aligning with the pragmatic design of this trial. Although originally validated in an HIV-infected population 20 , subsequent studies have shown good reliability when used as a patient-reported diary measure in the general population 22 .
[w3a3b1e-14] Secondary endpoints were the proportion of general practitioners who agreed to participate in the study, and the incidence of AEs and SAEs in each group. AEs and SAEs were recorded during follow-up calls and were coded according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA, version 28.1). Each AE was assessed for its relationship to the study treatment (probable, possible, unlikely, or unrelated) according to the criteria listed in the ICH E2A guidelines 23 .
[w3a3b1e-15] Sample size Sample size was calculated separately for each co-primary outcome, and the larger estimate was used. For the first co-primary outcome (time to 50% reduction in symptoms), we anticipated a one-day difference between groups and a standard deviation of three days, assumed to be equal across groups 21 . To detect this difference using a two-sample means test with 80% power and a two-sided significance level of 2.5%, 346 participants were required. For the second co-primary outcome (antibiotic use), based on prior literature 24 , we expected a 15–percentage-point (absolute) difference in the proportion of antibiotic use (i.e. 60% in usual care group and 45% in EPs®7630 group). To detect this difference using a two-sample proportions test (Pearson’s chi-squared test) with 80% power, a two-sided significance level of 2.5%, and 1:1 allocation, 420 participants (210 per group) were required. The sample size based on antibiotic use was therefore selected. This conservative approach did not adjust for potential correlations between outcomes or for participant drop-out.
[w3a3b1e-16] Statistical methods Statistical analyses were conducted in Stata (version 18). The randomisation group was used as the main predictor and analyses were adjusted for stratification factors (study centre) and pre-defined potential confounders based on clinical relevance, namely age, sex, level of education, smoking status, symptom duration at baseline, ABSS at baseline and use of steroids during the study period. All effects were shown using marginal point estimates, 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p -values. Missing data were imputed with multiple imputations by chained equations (MICE) under the assumption of missing at random. Patients were considered lost to follow-up if we were unable to reach them on day 22, the last day of follow-up. Adherence to treatment was defined as taking at least 50% of the prescribed doses during the period of symptoms, assessed for a maximum of 7 days as recommended in the monograph of the product. Primary and secondary analyses were conducted on the full analysis set (FAS) according to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principles, with participants analysed according to their assigned group. Per-protocol (PP) analyses were also performed to further investigate the effect of the intervention. PP population was defined as participants who did not have any intercurrent events (ICE), including non-respect of the treatment strategy (ICE1), inclusion despite presence of an exclusion criterion (ICE2) and terminal events, such as death (ICE3).
[w3a3b1e-17] The first co-primary endpoint (50% reduction in ABSS) was assessed using multiple linear regression. As data were positively skewed, log-transformation was used after adding the constant 1. For the second co-primary endpoint (proportion of antibiotics use), a Poisson regression was used to estimate risk ratios. To account for the fact that participants were clustered in general practices, bootstrapped standard errors (1000 reps) were used. For analyses on the PP set, inverse probability weighting (IPW) was used to re-balance randomisation groups, after exclusion of participants with ICEs. We handled missing values using MICE in the ITT analyses and simple imputation in the PP analyses, because balance may not be achieved when MICE is used along with IPW. Balance between groups (randomised groups and after IPW) was assessed using standardized mean/proportion differences (SMD), with values ≤ 0.25 considered acceptable 25 .
[w3a3b1e-18] The proportion of general practitioners who agreed to participate was reported with 95% CIs calculated using the Wilson method. AEs were analysed in the safety population, defined as all participants who received at least one dose of the study medication or usual care, following the ITT principles. The incidence of AEs and SAEs was summarised descriptively by treatment group. Between-group comparisons for the occurrence of at least one AE were performed using Cox proportional hazards models, adjusted for baseline covariates identified a priori, including age, sex, baseline ABSS, and recruitment site (walk-in clinic vs general practice). Results are presented as hazard ratios with 95% CIs and p values. Associations between baseline characteristics and the risk of AEs were explored using multivariable models with the same adjustment set.
[w3a3b1e-19] To test the trajectory of ABSS over time, a group-based trajectory model (GBTM) was applied to identify trajectories of symptom severity, with scores coded as 0 once symptom recovery was achieved. The number of trajectories was selected using usual fit indices: Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), loglikelihood, entropy, and the percentage of participants in each class ( ≥ 5%). A prespecified sensitivity analysis was conducted on the first co-primary endpoint (50% reduction in ABSS) using a negative-binomial regression. Additional Cox regression analyses were performed with randomisation group as the predictor: once for time to complete resolution of symptoms and once for time to antibiotics use.
[w3a3b1e-20] This trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov ( NCT05916768 ) on 23 June 2023.
[w3a3b1e-21] Results The study was terminated early due to budgetary constraints and recruitment challenges, partly because acute bronchitis occurs predominantly during winter months. Data from the 332 participants enrolled at the time of termination were included in the final analysis, instead of the 420 originally planned based on the sample size calculation. According to our sensitivity power analysis, this sample size provided power to detect medium effect sizes (supplementary File 1 ) 26 . Deviations from the protocol and the preregistered statistical analysis plan are detailed in the supplementary material (Table S.2. ; Supplementary File 2 ).
[w3a3b1e-22] Participant flow and baseline characteristics Participants were enrolled between Aug 16, 2023, and April 2, 2025, from 36 primary care practices (47 general practitioners) and from five walk-in clinics (42 general practitioners) across all French-speaking cantons of Switzerland. A total of 332 participants were randomly assigned and included in the ITT population: 155 in the EPs®7630 group and 177 in the usual care group (Fig. 1 ). After randomisation, seven participants withdrew and 95 were lost to follow-up. Fig. 1 Flowchart of the participants in the RCT.
[w3a3b1e-23] Baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups across all categories (standardised mean differences (SMD) ≤ 0.22), except for the number of medications prescribed at baseline (SMD 0.62; Table 1 ). The mean age was 41.2 years (IQR 18–87), 191 (57.5%) participants were women, and 240 (72.3%) were non-smokers. Thirty-seven (11.1%) participants reported having hypertension and nine (2.7%) diabetes. The mean baseline ABSS score was 11.3 (SD 3.6) out of 20, and the mean symptom duration before inclusion was 4.0 days (SD 1.9). Among participants in the usual care group, 166 (65.5%) were prescribed an antipyretic, 100 (62.1%) an anti-inflammatory, 101 (57.1%) an antitussive, 69 (39.0%) an expectorant, and 66 (37.3%) other medications, including saline solution for nasal use, nasal sprays and corticosteroids (nasal or oral). In the intervention group, 128 participants (82.6%) received additional medications alongside EPs®7630. The most frequently prescribed were antipyretics (104, 67.1%) and anti-inflammatory drugs (57, 36.8%) ( supplementary material , Table S.19 ). [w3a3b1e-27] Complete data were available for 276 (83.1%) participants for the co-primary outcome of time to 50% symptom reduction, for 246 (74.1%) participants for the co-primary outcome of antibiotic use, and for all participants for AEs. All 332 participants were included in the safety population because none reported not taking any medication. Medication data were missing for 39 participants; these individuals were assumed to have taken at least one symptom-reliever medication and were retained in the safety analysis.
[w3a3b1e-28] Co-primary outcomes There was no significant difference between the EPs®7630 and usual care groups in the time required to achieve a 50% reduction in symptoms. Both groups reached this milestone at a median of 4.0 days (EPs®7630 Interquartile Range (IQR) 4.0; usual care IQR 3.5). The adjusted regression coefficient (b) was 0.05 ([95% CI − 0.13 to 0.23]; p = 0.578), corresponding to an estimated difference of 0.11 days. Older age (b 0.01 [95% CI 0.00–0.01]; p = 0.020) and recruitment in a walk-in clinic (b 0.26 [95% CI 0.06–0.47]; p = 0.013) were significantly associated with a longer time to achieve a 50% symptom reduction (Table S.2. ).
[w3a3b1e-29] Antibiotic use was 7–percentage-points lower in the EPs®7630 group than in the usual care group, corresponding to a relative difference of 31%, but this difference was not statistically significant (risk ratio (RR) 0.78 [95% CI 0.49–1.26]; p = 0.309). During the study, 20 (17.4%) participants receiving EPs®7630 used antibiotics compared with 33 (25.2%) in the usual care group (Table 2 ). Delayed antibiotic prescriptions were provided to 25 participants in total, 12 in the intervention group and 13 in the control group. Among them, 18 participants (6 in the intervention group and 12 in the control group) ultimately used antibiotics. However, we did not collect data allowing us to determine whether antibiotic use resulted from the delayed prescription or from a subsequent medical consultation leading to a new prescription. A higher baseline ABSS was associated with a higher likelihood of antibiotic use (RR 1.43 [95% CI 1.11–1.84]; p = 0.006), whereas recruitment in a walk-in clinic was associated with a lower likelihood of antibiotic use (RR 0.45 [95% CI 0.25–0.82]; p = 0.010); (Table S.3. ). [w3a3b1e-32] Secondary outcomes Regarding secondary outcomes, 89 of the 126 general practitioners invited to participate in the study agreed to take part (70.6%; Wilson 95% CI 62.2–77.9). Overall, 88 (26.5%) participants experienced at least one AE during the trial. AEs were reported more frequently in the EPs®7630 group than in the usual care group (50 [32.3%] of 155 versus 38 [21.5%] of 177; hazard ratio (HR) 1.73 [95% CI 1.13–2.63]; p = 0.011; Table 2 ). AEs were classified as probably caused by study treatment in 15.8% of participants in the EPs®7630 group and 34.0% in the usual care group, possible in 78.9% vs 58.0%, unlikely in 1.8% vs 2.1%, and unrelated in 3.5% vs 2.1%, respectively (Table S.4. ). Most AEs were mild gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea or diarrhoea. Fatigue, headache, and vertigo were more frequent in the usual care group (30.0%, 10.0%, and 4.0%, respectively) than in the EPs®7630 group (14.0%, 7.0%, and 0%), whereas other AEs, such as gastrointestinal symptoms or gingivitis, were reported more frequently in the EPs®7630 group. A higher baseline ABSS was associated with a greater likelihood of developing an AE (hazard ratio 1.21 [95% CI 1.04–1.41]; p = 0.015; table S.5. ). Two SAEs occurred, both in the usual care group; neither was related to the study, as both were elective hospitalisations for planned procedures unrelated to bronchitis.
[w3a3b1e-33] Exploratory analyses Five symptom trajectories were identified using GBTM (Fig. 2 ). Trajectory 1 showed the fastest recovery, whereas trajectory 5 showed the slowest. Participants were distributed across all trajectories, while trajectory 4 included the largest proportion of participants (27.2%). There were no significant differences in trajectory distribution between groups (Table S.6. ). Overall, 61 participants (30.2%) reconsulted their general practitioner during their episode of acute bronchitis, whereas 141 (69.8%) did not; data for this analysis were missing for 130 participants. Repeat consultation within 22 days was positively correlated with antibiotic use (r = 0.50; p < 0.001). PP and sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the main findings (Table S.8. – S.19. ; Figure S.1. and S.2. ), with results consistent with the primary analyses. [w3a3b1e-34] Discussion Phytobronch is the first pragmatic, open-label, randomised controlled trial led by an independent academic institution to assess the effectiveness of EPs®7630 for acute bronchitis. We found that EPs®7630 did not shorten symptom duration compared with usual care, but was associated with a 7–percentage-point lower rate of antibiotic use, a difference that was not statistically significant, but could be clinically relevant. AEs were more frequently reported in the EPs®7630 group, though all were mild and self-limiting. Two SAEs occurred, both in the usual care group, and were deemed unrelated to any study treatment. This trial is the first independent, real-world evaluation of EPs®7630 in primary care—the setting where most patients with acute bronchitis are diagnosed and treated.
[w3a3b1e-35] Three previous randomised controlled trials including a total of 746 adults are published and report that participants receiving EPs®7630 had lower symptom severity at day 7 compared with placebo 14 – 16 . In one of these trials 16 , 19 , participants receiving EPs®7630 also had a median of two fewer days off work than those receiving placebo. At least two trials evaluating EPs®7630 for acute bronchitis in adults remain unpublished 19 , limiting the completeness of the available evidence. Our pragmatic trial found no evidence that EPs®7630 shortened the time to 50% symptom reduction compared with usual care. Because the trial was not placebo-controlled, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting the overall effectiveness of the intervention in routine care rather than the intrinsic efficacy of EPs®7630. Although evidence supporting the efficacy of treatments such as antipyretics, non-steroidal anti-inflammatories, and antitussives for this self-limiting condition is limited 6 , such treatments may nonetheless provide some perceived symptom relief compared with a placebo. Furthermore, the absence of blinding in our trial could have introduced a degree of reporting bias in either direction. Pragmatic trials typically yield smaller effect sizes than placebo-controlled studies but provide a more accurate estimate of real-world effectiveness, reflecting everyday clinical practice where blinding, adherence control, and treatment standardisation are limited 27 . Similar findings have been reported in pragmatic trials in other conditions, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, where effect sizes were smaller than in previous placebo-controlled trials 28 .
[w3a3b1e-36] This trial was the first to assess antibiotic use as a co-primary outcome, providing additional insight into the persistent challenge of inappropriate antibiotic prescribing. EPs®7630 was associated with an absolute reduction of 7% in antibiotic use, corresponding to a relative difference of 31% between groups. Although this difference was not statistically significant, it could be clinically relevant. The lack of statistical significance could be explained by limited power, resulting from a smaller-than-planned sample size and a lower rate of antibiotic use than expected. Only 20% of participants in our trial used antibiotics, compared with around 40% reported previously for acute bronchitis in Switzerland 4 . This difference likely reflects a healthy participant bias: trial participants were healthier than the general Swiss adult population, with lower reported rates of hypertension and diabetes (11.1% versus 20% and 2.7% versus 5%, respectively). In addition, participation bias among general practitioners may have contributed, as participating clinicians could already have lower rates of inappropriate antibiotic prescribing than their peers. Also, awareness raised during study initiation—where the non-indication of antibiotics for acute bronchitis was emphasized—may also have reinforced prudent prescribing practices 29 . Previous European recommendations, including those from the European Surveillance of Antimicrobial Consumption, suggest antibiotic prescribing thresholds for acute bronchitis ranging from 13% to 30% 30 , 31 . Current prescribing rates in Switzerland therefore exceed these thresholds by at least 10%. In this context, the observed absolute reduction of 7% in antibiotic use is clinically meaningful and suggests that EPs®7630 could contribute to reducing inappropriate antibiotic use, supporting antimicrobial stewardship efforts in primary care.
[w3a3b1e-37] General practitioners frequently perceive pressure from patients to prescribe antibiotics, which are commonly thought to be effective 32 . Because symptomatic treatments have limited evidence of benefit 6 , and antibiotics are not recommended for the treatment of acute viral bronchitis, clinicians are often left in a difficult position. To address this challenge, several strategies have been developed to reduce antibiotic prescribing for acute respiratory infections, including point-of-care testing (POCT) and delayed antibiotic prescriptions, both of which have demonstrated effectiveness 33 , 34 . However, the implementation of these strategies in routine practice remains variable 35 , and many patients still expect to receive an antibiotic or symptomatic medication when consulting a general practitioner 36 , 37 . In light of the persistent overuse of antibiotics, despite existing stewardship strategies, the use of EPs®7630 may represent a potentially alternative. This interpretation is supported by findings from a retrospective cohort study of 117 182 outpatients in Germany, in which the use of P. sidoides for upper and lower respiratory tract infections was associated with significantly lower antibiotic prescribing 38 . From a safety standpoint, although more adverse events were reported in the EPs®7630 group than with usual care, all were mild and aligned with the established safety profile described in the literature. Reporting bias is also likely, as participants receiving the intervention may have monitored and reported symptoms more closely. Symptom-relieving options such as EPs®7630 may therefore serve as a useful adjunct within a comprehensive antibiotic stewardship strategy, particularly for patients seeking plant-based alternatives 39 , 40 . Nevertheless, this potential benefit should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger trials. Other herbal products such as Echinacea preparations have been reported to reduce antibiotic use for respiratory tract infections, suggesting that some herbal products may contribute to stewardship efforts 41 . However, such an approach would be meaningful only when accompanied by broader measures to support rational prescribing, including clinician and patient education 1 .
[w3a3b1e-38] Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the open-label design, without blinding of participants or general practitioners, could have introduced bias. For example, differences in reported AEs may reflect reporting bias, as participants in the intervention group might have paid greater attention to symptoms 42 . Nevertheless, this pragmatic, unblinded design provides real-world primary care practice, where patients are aware of the treatments they receive. This approach is particularly relevant for self-limiting conditions such as acute bronchitis, where symptom relief—rather than objective biological markers—is the outcome that matters most. Secondly, the study is underpowered to detect true differences between groups. Consequently, the observed effects may be attributable to chance, due both to a smaller-than-planned sample size and to smaller-than-expected effect sizes. For instance, the difference in antibiotic use between groups was less pronounced than anticipated. In addition, the high dropout rate may have reduced the precision of the estimates, although missing data were handled using multiple imputation. Dropouts may be explained by technical issues, as some participants did not receive the link to complete the questionnaire due to incorrect email addresses or phone numbers. It is also possible that completing the daily diary was perceived as too burdensome by some participants. Our study was powered to detect medium effect size, but small effect sizes could be clinically relevant. Thirdly, our participants had a lower burden of chronic disease than the general population, consistent with the exclusion of individuals with immunosuppression or anticoagulant treatment. This healthier profile may partly explain the lower overall antibiotic use compared with previous national reports 4 , and may limit the generalisability of our findings to populations with higher multimorbidity. Our findings require replication in larger, adequately powered trials in order to provide more precise estimates to inform clinical practice.
[w3a3b1e-39] In conclusion, EPs®7630 did not reduce symptom duration or significantly reduce antibiotic use compared with usual care among adults with acute bronchitis. Although the observed reduction in antibiotic prescribing did not reach statistical significance, it merits further evaluation in adequately powered trials to better define any potential contribution to antimicrobial stewardship. Given the ongoing overuse of antibiotics despite established stewardship measures, adjunctive approaches, including selected herbal products, deserve careful, evidence-based investigation as possible components of future stewardship strategies.
