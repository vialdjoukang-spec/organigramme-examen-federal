Severe colitis is a well-defined condition encompassing several etiologies but is most often caused by severe ulcerative colitis or Clostridioides difficile infection. Severe colitis can evolve into toxic colitis, or toxic megacolon when associated with bowel dilation and systemic manifestations, resulting in a life-threatening scenario where multidisciplinary management is often required. Medical management continues to play an important role in the initial treatment of toxic megacolon. However, timely surgical intervention can be lifesaving.

The diagnostic criteria and screening scores for sepsis, such as the systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) criteria, quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score, and Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS), were validated. The preoperative clinical features and perioperative findings were also investigated.
[w37cda0-4] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) Results: There were eight male and six female patients. Nine patients (64.3%) satisfied the criteria for toxemia proposed by Narabayashi, and 10 patients (71.4%) fulfilled the SIRS criteria. A positive qSOFA score was confirmed in 1 patient (7.1%). [w37cda0-5] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) Conclusions: TM does not have well-defined diagnostic criteria, in addition to developing sometimes as borderline or fulminant cases, and must be recognized at an early stage, taking various findings into consideration. [w37cda0-6] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) TM is diagnosed on the basis of findings from a number of examinations, such as vital signs, X-ray studies, blood tests, and sigmoidoscopy, and there is no modality that alone clinches the diagnosis easily[ 5 ]. However, TM must be recognized at an initial stage and treated adequately because it has a risk of worsening rapidly. Early identification and intensive management may help reduce the incidence and mortality of TM in inflammatory bowel disease[ 6 ]. In the present study, we reviewed cases of TM treated in our department, summarized the timing and technique of surgery, and considered key points for surgical management of TM.
[w37cda0-7] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) Methods This single-center retrospective study recruited the medical records of patients who were clinically diagnosed with TM (a condition in which colonic ileus is accompanied by sepsis) due to UC and underwent surgery for TM between July 1985 and March 2020 at our hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Advisory Committee of Fukuoka University Chikushi Hospital (No. C21-01-013) and conformed to the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki. Consent for the use of date for this research was obtained on an optout basis ( https://www.chikushi.fukuoka-u.ac.jp/rinshou/patient/ushiromuki.html ).
[w37cda0-8] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) We made a diagnosis of colonic ileus when a patient's abdominal X-ray findings in the supine position demonstrated dilatation, especially in the transverse colon[ 7 ]. A clinical diagnosis of “toxemia” was validated on the criteria reported by Jalan[ 8 ] and Narabayashi[ 9 ]. Jalan et al. reported that a patient was considered to be “toxic,” when they showed any 3 of the 4 criteria in group A (pyrexia >101.5°F [38.6°C], tachycardia >120 beats/minute, leukocytosis >105,000/mm 3 , and anemia <60% hemoglobin) together with any 1 of the 4 criteria in group B (dehydration, mental changes, electrolyte disturbance, hypotension). According to the description of Narabayashi, the criteria of the diagnosis for “systemic toxicity” included at least 2 of the following: pyrexia (>38.6°C), tachycardia (>100 beats/minute), leukocytosis (>10,500/μL), or hypoalbuminemia (<3.0 g/dL).
[w37cda0-9] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) We also checked which patients met the criteria for Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) and measured their quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score and Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS). SIRS is a clinical response of the host to inflammation[ 10 ]. The qSOFA is a screening tool that can detect sepsis at an early stage in adult patients[ 11 , 12 ]. The MEWS is a simple bedside tool that can identify patients at risk of deterioration and in need of more active intervention[ 13 ]. SIRS is defined in cases with any 2 of the following: a body temperature >38°C or <36°C, a heart rate >90 beats/minute, respiratory rate >20 breaths/minute, and white blood cell count >12,000/mm 3 , <4,000/mm 3 , or >10% immature neutrophils (“bands”)[ 10 ]. Adult patients were considered more likely to have poor outcomes typical of sepsis if they had at least 2 of the following clinical criteria that together constitute the qSOFA score: a respiratory rate of ≥22 breaths/minute, altered consciousness, and a systolic blood pressure of ≤100 mmHg[ 12 ]. The MEWS was calculated based on a previously published table[ 13 ]. Each criterion is described in Table 1 .

[w37cda0-13] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) Results There were 288 patients who were conducted surgery for UC at our hospital between 1985 and 2020. Fourteen patients clinically diagnosed with TM due to UC and underwent surgery were included in this study. None of patients diagnosed with TM were not treated surgically. Table 2 shows the patients' clinical characteristics and preoperative course. There were eight male and six female patients, and the median (range) age at the onset of TM was 39 (19-75) years old. The median duration from the diagnosis of UC to surgery for TM was 5 (1-239) months. All patients were confirmed to have dilatation of ≥6 cm in the transverse colon by plain abdominal X-ray in the supine position ( Figure 1 ). The median time until surgery from the TM diagnosis was 3 (0-57) days. Emergency surgery was performed in 13 of 14 patients (92.9%). Glucocorticoid therapy was administered to all patients, and the median total prednisolone dose was 1,500 (120-30,000) mg. Four patients underwent cytapheresis.

[w37cda0-22] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) Intestinal perforation occurred in 2 patients (14.3%), with both cases occurring 6 days after the diagnosis of TM. One patient without perforation was able to avoid emergency surgery because the transanal tube successfully decompressed the distended colon. Eleven patients (78.6%) underwent total colectomy with the formation of end ileostomy, and 3 patients underwent total proctocolectomy, ileal pouch anal anastomosis, and diverting ileostomy. The median operative time and blood loss were 147.5 (90-216) minutes and 506 (61-1,900) ml, respectively. Postoperative complications occurred in 9 of 14 patients (64.3%), and 1 patient required unplanned surgical intervention for bleeding. Among all patients investigated, there was 1 death during the postoperative hospital stay, with a 30-day mortality rate of 7.1%. One of two patients with perforation died of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to sepsis. The mortality rate of patients with TM and perforation was 50%. The median post-operative hospital stay of patients managed successfully was 32 (17-60) days.
[w37cda0-23] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) Discussion In this single-center retrospective study, we reviewed cases of TM treated in our department, summarized the timing and technique of surgery, and considered key points for surgical management of TM. TM is a severe and potentially lethal complication of UC as well as perforation, profuse bleeding, cancer, or high-grade dysplasia[ 1 - 4 ]. The pathophysiology of TM is thought to be colonic ileus accompanied by sepsis[ 14 , 15 ], but Michael et al. stated that TM does not have well-defined limits[ 16 ].
[w37cda0-24] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) Gan et al. reported that dilatation of ≥6 cm in the transverse colon on plain abdominal X-ray is suggestive of the diagnosis of megacolon[ 17 ], while Narabayashi described megacolon as ≥5-6 cm of dilatation of the colon on X-ray[ 9 ]. In addition, the upper limit of the normal diameter of the transverse colon has been defined as 5.5 cm[ 14 , 18 ]. Given these facts, the characteristic colon diameter in cases of TM seems uncertain. In the present study, all patients showed dilatation of ≥6 cm in the transverse colon on plain abdominal radiography. We therefore also believe that TM is likely when the transverse colon shows dilation of ≥6.0.
[w37cda0-25] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) Several criteria concerning a toxemic state have been proposed, as have those for megacolon, but toxicity is reportedly difficult to define[ 5 ]. We validated the criteria of toxicity advocated by Jalan[ 8 ] and Narabayashi[ 9 ] in our patients and also checked whether or not the patients showed positive screening scores for sepsis or a critical MEWS. Although the criteria of Jalan and Narabayashi and screening scores for sepsis or deterioration were considered as possible indicators to evaluate the “toxicity”, all indicators were not applicable to all the 14 cases of TM surgically treated in this study. The qSOFA showed higher overall prognostic accuracy than the SIRS criteria[ 19 ], and the MEWS also had higher specificity for predicting in-hospital mortality[ 20 ]. However, the SIRS criteria have high sensitivity for predicting systemic inflammation and are maintained as a screening tool for early care and the prevention of a missed diagnosis[ 19 - 21 ]. It seems preferable that a screening tool with a high sensitivity be used to identify TM in order to avoid losing the opportunity to perform properly indicated surgical intervention. The criteria proposed by Narabayashi and the SIRS criteria may be recommended to be used to determine “toxicity”, because these criteria were met in a high percentage of our cases.
[w37cda0-26] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) Typical abdominal symptoms may not present in TM patients complicated with UC, due to the masking effect of steroid therapy[ 6 ]. Indeed, 3 of 14 patients (21.4%) in the present study did not satisfy any toxic criteria or show expected screening scores for sepsis. If immunosuppressed patients suffer from bacteremia due to bacterial translocation, their hemodynamic and respiratory status will rapidly become compromised. In borderline cases, it may become necessary to perform assessments repeatedly and entirely rely on clinical judgment without fixating on criteria for the diagnosis of TM[ 5 ].
[w37cda0-27] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) Surgical intervention is indicated if initial conservative management, such as correction of fluids, hydroelectrolytic disorders, anemia, broad-spectrum antibiotics, endovenous corticosteroids, and intubation with an intestinal tube, fails to improve clinical findings within 24-72 h after the onset. Protracted medical management may lead to perforation, and it has been reported that the incidence of perforation in TM is 24%[ 16 ]. It has been reported that the mortality rate of TM patients is <10% if surgery is performed without perforation, but it increases to 40%-50% in cases of perforation[ 22 - 24 ]. Similar to the literature findings, our patients with both TM and intestinal perforation had a poor prognosis. Surgery should always be considered, and patients should be informed early that surgical intervention is necessary if clinical findings are refractory to medical management[ 25 ].
[w37cda0-28] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) Relatively simple surgical procedures are preferred in emergency cases of TM, as the patient's general condition is unstable[ 14 , 16 ]. There is also a high risk of peritoneal contamination during surgery, as handling of the massively distended bowel is difficult[ 26 ], as was noted on the intraoperative view of a patient in the present study ( Figure 2 ). However, diverting stoma formation alone is reportedly an inadequate procedure in such cases[ 16 ]. Given the previous findings, total colectomy with terminal ileostomy seems to be the preferred operative procedure in emergency cases[ 14 , 16 , 25 ].

[w37cda0-30] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) Several limitations associated with the present study warrant mention. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small number of patients compared with other review articles. In addition, there were many missing values regarding the respiratory rate. We must accept that examinations of toxicity, especially the SIRS criteria, qSOFA, and MEWS, can be inaccurate. However, even if the patients with missing data in our study had a manifested respiratory rate that satisfied the qSOFA criteria or a high MEWS, the SIRS criteria would still have been met in most patients in this study.
[w37cda0-31] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=11, 2026-11-10) There are a few reports of TM secondary to inflammatory bowel disease being successfully treated with non-surgical management, but those might have been rare cases in a limited situation[ 9 , 27 - 29 ]. The mortality rate increases remarkably if intestinal perforation complicates TM. Our results support the idea that surgical intervention should always be considered from the initial stage. In conclusion, it is important to consider surgery in the early stages of toxic megacolon, even if symptoms and clinical findings do not meet all the criteria. Toxic Megacolon: Background, Pathophysiology, Management Challenges and Solutions [Corrigendum] 14 19 7 2021 309 310 Clinical and Experimental Gastroenterology 10.2147/CEG.S329394 PMC8302810 34321902 Abstract Toxic megacolon (TM) is one of the fatal complications of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or any infectious etiology of the colon that is characterized by total or partial nonobstructive colonic dilatation and systemic toxicity. It is associated with high morbidity and mortality, and surgical management is necessary ﻿for the majority of the cases. An accurate history and physical examination, plain radiographs of the abdomen, sigmoidoscopy, and, most important of all, awareness of the condition facilitate diagnosis in most cases. Operative intervention is warranted when massive hemorrhage, perforation, or peritonitis complicate the clinical scenario or medical therapy fails to control the disease. We sought to review the management challenges of TM and its possible management strategies in this article.
[w128cab-2] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Keywords toxic megacolon inflammatory bowel disease management challenges and solutions for toxic megacolon Clostridium difficile colitis nitric oxide synthase pmc-status-qastatus 0 pmc-status-live yes pmc-status-embargo no pmc-status-released yes pmc-prop-open-access yes pmc-prop-olf no pmc-prop-manuscript no pmc-prop-legally-suppressed no pmc-prop-has-pdf yes pmc-prop-has-supplement no pmc-prop-pdf-only no pmc-prop-suppress-copyright no pmc-prop-is-real-version no pmc-prop-is-scanned-article no pmc-prop-preprint no pmc-prop-in-epmc yes pmc-license-ref CC BY-NC Introduction Toxic megacolon (TM) is a potentially fatal condition defined as an acute colonic dilatation, greater than 6 cm in diameter, of the transverse colon, and loss of haustration on radiologic examination in a case of severe colitis. 1 , 2 Despite its low prevalence, the outcomes are still unsatisfactory, with in-hospital mortality of 7.9%. 3 , 4 Our review aims are to highlight the crucial aspects and recent advancements regarding epidemiology, pathogenesis, and management of TM.
[w128cab-3] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Epidemiology The exact prevalence of TM is underreported. However, the prevalence continues to increase with age. The most common cause of hospital admission included IBD (51.6%), followed by septicemia (10.2%) and intestinal infections (4.1%). 4 Some studies reported that the incidence of TM is higher in patients with Ulcerative Colitis (UC) compared to Crohn’s disease (CD) (8–10% in UC compared to 2.3% in CD). 5 , 6 In contrast, others reported its incidence in CD to be higher than in UC (4.4–6.3% in CD compared to 1–2.5% in UC). 7 Incidence of TM due to Clostridium difficile ( C. diff ) was estimated to be 0.4–3% before 1990; however, it is 4.3% after 1990. 5 , 6 Female gender, age more than 40 years, hypoalbuminemia, acidosis, and high blood urea nitrogen levels are associated with high mortality in a previous study. 8 The mortality rate in patients with TM is variable. Before 1976, the mortality rate for TM was 27% in medically managed cases, however, as low as 19% in surgically managed cases, which dropped dramatically to a mere 0–2% in patients with IBD. Early identification and intensive management may have contributed to the reduced incidence and mortality of TM in IBD. 9 Patients with fulminant infection require surgical intervention in up to 20% of cases, carrying mortality rates between 35 and 80%. 10 , 11 Colonic perforation is one of the most important predictors of mortality; 44% of patients underwent emergent colectomy after perforation, while only 2% of patients without perforation needed the procedure. 8
[w128cab-4] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Etiology UC and CD are important etiologies for TM making it evident that any inflammatory condition of the colon could predispose to TM. 5 , 25 , 26 TM is a rare but potentially lethal complication of any disease that causes inflammation of the colon, including IBD and Infectious etiologies such as C. diff and cytomegalovirus colitis. 5 , 27 , 28 Other precipitating factors for TM include anti-motility agents, such as anticholinergic medications and narcotics, electrolyte derangements, such as hypokalemia, and diagnostic procedures like barium enema and colonoscopy. 29
[w128cab-5] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Pathogenesis The Massachusetts General Hospital reported its isolated cases of TM from in 1933, 1936, and 1941. 12 In 1956, Bockus et al drew attention to it in their study. They described it as “toxic aganglionic megacolon,” referring to what they suggested about its pathogenesis, which was described as destructive changes in the nerve plexus in the distal colon. 13 The mechanism involved in the development of TM remains unclear; however, changes in colonic response to chemical mediators result in defective smooth muscle contraction and lowered basal pressure of the colonic lumen, which may play an essential role in the development of TM. 14 – 17 NO is an inhibitory neurotransmitter that is known to cause relaxation of the colonic smooth muscles. 18 NO synthase is expressed more in the inflamed mucosa of UC patients compared to noninflamed mucosa, which subsequently leads to suppression of colonic contraction. 19 – 21 Selective inhibition of NO synthase reportedly improves the motility, diameter, and intracolonic pressure in animal models with colitis. 22 Enemas containing NO synthase inhibitor revealed a clinically significant benefit in a patient with TM due to UC. 23 In contrast to what was suggested by Bockus et al, the inflammation in the case of TM is reportedly caused by the neuromuscular transmission rather than degeneration of the neurons. 24
[w128cab-6] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Clinical Manifestations TM affects both gender and all ages; however, complications can present earlier in patients with IBD. TM occurs in around 5% of a severe attack of UC. Approximately half of the patients with TM develop this complication in the first three months of their IBD diagnosis. 30 TM usually occurs in patients with pancolitis, but patients with only left-sided colitis also can develop it. The most common presenting symptom is severe bloody diarrhea. With the clinical decline, patients can also develop hypotension, tachycardia, fever, diffuse abdominal tenderness with distention, and sluggish bowel sounds. One study revealed that patients with TM secondary to C. diff colitis had diarrhea as a complaint in all cases, abdominal pain, and distention in up to 82 percent of the cases and the malaise in 91 percent of the cases. 31 Other laboratory parameters showing progressive systemic toxicity include significant leukocytosis, metabolic alkalosis, and electrolyte derangements.
[w128cab-7] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Nevertheless, strong analgesics or altered mentation might conceal the signs or symptoms of TM. Free perforation can also occur without dilatation of the colon. It is a rare complication, developing in approximately 1% of patients with UC without TM. 32 , 33 Typical presentation of peritonitis may be absent, primarily due to the masking effect of steroid therapy. It is crucial to look for hepatic dullness daily, especially in patients with severe colitis, and are on steroid therapy as they might have a free perforation and not have typical signs of peritonitis. For the same reason, a daily x-ray of the abdomen is recommended.
[w128cab-8] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Diagnosis As per the description of Jalan et al, the diagnostic criteria of TM include (a) radiographic evidence of colonic dilatation of more than 6 cm especially in the transverse colon; (b) any 3 of the following: fever (> 38.6 °C, 101.5 °F), tachycardia (> 120 beats/min), leukocytosis (> 10.5 × 103/μL), or anemia; and (c) any of the following: hypotension, hypovolemia, altered mental status, or electrolyte disorders. 12 The diagnostic purpose of TM is mainly for the diagnosis of the condition and the underlying etiology. Imaging studies are required for the diagnosis of TM as a “megacolon” is defined radiographically by a maximum diameter of >6 cm. Computed tomography of the abdomen with contrast helps establish the diagnosis and also evaluating for complications that may require immediate surgery. Baseline and serial abdominal x rays help follow the progression of colonic dilatation. Typical features might include dilated transverse or right colon (more than 6 cm) more frequently compared to descending colon, sigmoid colon or rectum; deep mucosal ulcerations, segmental colonic wall thinning, air-fluid levels with abnormal haustral pattern and nodular pseudo polyps on radiologic exam. Common nonspecific lab abnormalities associated with TM include leukocytosis with prominent neutrophilia, especially in C. diff colitis cases, anemia from gastrointestinal blood loss, metabolic alkalosis secondary to volume depletion, hypokalemia, hypoalbuminemia and elevated inflammatory markers including ESR and CRP.
[w128cab-9] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) A complete colonoscopy is extremely risky in patients with TM because it can cause colonic perforation. A limited endoscopic examination without bowel preparation (eg, proctoscopy or sigmoidoscopy) is safer compared to complete colonoscopy. It is useful to diagnose an inflammatory (eg, [IBD]) or infectious process (eg, cytomegalovirus [CMV] or C. diff colitis) in the rectum or sigmoid colon. Nevertheless, a sigmoidoscopy can miss CMV inclusion bodies as classic ulcers are generally present in the ascending colon in a majority of the cases. 34
[w128cab-10] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Treatment The main objective for the treatment of TM is to reduce the inflammation, improve the motility of the colon, and to prevent free perforation. If UC gets complicated with colonic dilatation, fluid replacement and intravenous steroid therapy is recommended, along with addressing electrolyte derangements aggressively. Bowel rest and nasogastric decompression can also help in the reduction of air and fluid within the GI tract. Both medical and surgical teams should co-manage patients on admission with daily evaluation.
[w128cab-11] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Inpatient evaluation, preferably in the closed observation unit, with periodic examinations, help assess for signs of toxicity. Serial labs, including CBC and Electrolytes, along with clinical abdominal x-ray, should be performed twice a day initially upon admission and subsequently daily as the condition improves. Maneuvers to decrease abdominal distension by permitting the redistribution or passing of colon gas could be another conservative management strategy. Encouraging patients to walk around, using knee-elbow maneuver in the prone position, rotating patient who is bed-bound and inserting a rectal tube helps reducing bowel distension. 30 Enteral feeding can be initiated as soon as the patient shows signs of improvement to promote gut motility. Total parental nutrition (TPN) is of limited value in patients with TM from any cause. TPN provides no proven clinical benefit in terms of avoiding surgical intervention in patients with colitis due to UC. 35 When it comes to the specific medical or surgical therapy for TM must target the underlying etiology:
[w128cab-12] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Inflammatory bowel disease — 5-ASA compounds and Sulfasalazine have no role in patients with IBD-related TM and should be commenced only after the acute attack resolves. In a few cases, an acute attack of TM might co-occur with the initiation of 5-ASA compounds.
[w128cab-13] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Glucocorticoids Intravenous glucocorticoids (hydrocortisone 100 mg every 6 to 8 hours) are the mainstay of therapy for all patients with TM secondary to IBD and are not associated with a higher risk of perforation. 36 However, this approach is based on limited observational data and clinical experience. Dexamethasone, by diminishing the expression of NO synthase, has been reported to decrease the colonic diameter. Most providers consider methylprednisolone because of its lower potassium wasting and sodium retaining properties, while others prefer prednisolone as the oral and parenteral doses are the same.
[w128cab-14] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Infliximab or Cyclosporine Patients with IBD-related TM who are refractory to three days of intravenous glucocorticoid therapy should receive either Infliximab or Cyclosporine as the second-line therapy. Infliximab is the preferred second-line treatment for all patients with IBD-related TM. Cyclosporine should be reserved for those who cannot tolerate Infliximab, and there is only evidence for its effectiveness in UC, not CD. (See Table 1 ) Data suggest that cyclosporine may provide an initial response rate of as high as 80%. After a follow-up period, the response rate decreases to approximately 40%. Although further studies are required, cyclosporine therapy may prevent the need for urgent surgery, allowing an elective surgery to be performed under the more controlled scenario. Most patients who develop IBD-related TM have UC; some have indeterminate colitis or Crohn’s colitis. Since these three conditions may not be readily distinguishable during an acute flare-up such as TM, many authors suggest treating all IBD-related TM with the same approach. Steroids are contraindicated in TM due to an infectious etiology, including C. diff colitis.
[w128cab-20] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Empiric antibiotics are indicated as mortality due to TM correlates with the development of sepsis. Approximately half of the acute dilatation cases resolve with medical therapy alone. 32 Perforation of colon is the most critical predictor of mortality (44% of cases require emergent surgical intervention following perforation vs only 2% of cases requiring it without perforation), 9 a critical aspect of management is to decide the optimal time for surgical intervention. Generally, cases that do not improve after 2–3 days of medical management require a surgical evaluation. 41
[w128cab-21] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Surgery is indicated in patients with colonic perforation, necrosis, or full-thickness ischemia, intra-abdominal hypertension or abdominal compartment syndrome, clinical signs of peritonitis, or worsening abdominal exam despite adequate medical therapy, and end-organ failure. Besides, white blood cell count >50,000 cell/mL and serum lactate level of >5 mmol/L are relative indications for surgical intervention. The timing of surgery in TM is still a matter of controversy and varies by the underlying etiology of the TM. Although the main objective of all medical therapy is to avoid the need for surgical management, delaying surgery may ultimately increase the risk of complications such as abdominal compartment syndrome or perforation. Careful monitoring for any signs of impending perforation is vital. Patients developing abdominal distention, rebound tenderness, or hemodynamic instability require emergent surgical intervention. Further management is debatable for patients who achieve remission with medical management. According to one study, almost half of the patients who were managed successfully for TM eventually required surgical intervention. 42 Because of these facts, some providers offer elective surgical evaluation after the resolution of an acute attack of TM. Early surgical intervention before colonic perforation has a lower mortality rate compared to colectomy after perforation (8 versus 40 percent). 9 (See Table 2 ). Patients with cytomegalovirus (CMV) colitis or C. diff infection respond poorly to medical management only and often require surgical intervention. 49 , 50 Critically ill patients with advanced AIDS who are poor surgical candidates could be managed by supportive therapy, antibiotics, and careful colonic decompression.
[w128cab-27] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Pregnant women Women with known risk factors for TM (most commonly UC) should plan conception during a state of remission. 51 Patients who are in remission at the conception are likely to remain in remission during pregnancy. In the case of TM in pregnancy primarily due to UC, high-dose intravenous glucocorticoids therapy is effective in three-fourth cases of severe colitis, the remainder requiring either Infliximab or emergent surgical intervention. 52 , 53
[w128cab-28] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Management of fulminant colitis during pregnancy is challenging as the gravid uterus can restrict a thorough abdominal examination, and diagnostic radiation could be teratogenic. 54 In pregnancy, hemoglobin, and serum albumin level drop, while other inflammatory markers, including the ESR and CRP rise; so, these blood tests are not reliable measures of disease activity. Urgent colon surgery can increase the risks of preterm delivery and spontaneous abortions.
[w128cab-29] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Prognosis The mortality rates for TM are inconsistent. Before 1976, the death rate in 604 cases of TM was 27 percent (43 out of 160) for patients who were managed medically and 19 percent in 444 patients who underwent surgical intervention. 55 The in-hospital mortality for TM further decreased from 9.2 to 6.5 percent in 2010 through 2014. 4 Colonic perforation is associated with a significantly worse prognosis, with the mortality rate increased by three- to fivefold. 6 , 8
[w128cab-30] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) The etiology of TM can also affect the prognosis, TM secondary to infectious etiology has a better prognosis than patients with TM secondary to IBD who have been treated with biologics; however, this result is mainly based upon a small group of studies. The prognosis is especially good when colonic dilatation complicates acute self-limited colitis and is managed promptly. 56 Nevertheless, the mortality rates dropped significantly to less than 2 percent in patients with IBD, probably related to diverse factors, including early identification of TM, the rapid escalation from glucocorticoid therapy to biologics (or cyclosporine), increasing use of biologics and accelerated dosing, aggressive medical management, prompt surgical intervention, and better post-surgical care. 9
[w128cab-31] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) A review of patients with TM due to C. diff infection between 1968 and 1992 revealed an overall mortality of 31 to 35 percent. 31 In other studies, the overall mortality from TM secondary to severe C. diff infection was 64 to 67 percent, and 71 to 100 percent in cases which were managed surgically. 31 , 57 These studies show that patients with TM due to C. diff infection will do better with medical management alone, and surgery may offer a nominal benefit. However, surgery should not be postponed in acutely sick patients.
[w128cab-32] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) The difference in mortality rates may also be due to the biases of medical or surgical providers. Two studies have revealed that 68 to 75 percent of cases of TM managed medically did not require a surgical intervention later and have maintained remission for up to six years. 6 , 9 Surgical studies reveal up to a 50 percent rate of future surgical intervention, including colectomy in patients with TM who initially responded well to medical treatment alone. 55 , 58 Timely surgical interventions decreased the mortality rate significantly from 22 to 1.2 percent in one report. 59 The key points of this review article have been summarized in Table 3 . [w128cab-33] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Key points of the Review Article: 1. Toxic megacolon (TM) was thought to be a complication for ulcerative colitis (UC) specifically. Later on, the Crohn’s disease (CD) was found to be a cause, and gradually it becomes evident that any inflammatory condition of the colon could predispose to TM. 2. Accurate history and physical examination, plain radiographs of the abdomen, sigmoidoscopy and, most important of all, awareness of the condition facilitate diagnosis in most cases. 3. The most common cause of hospital admission included Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (51.6%), followed by septicemia (10.2%) and intestinal infections (4.1%).
[w128cab-34] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) 4. Computed tomography of the abdomen with contrast is usually performed to establish the diagnosis and also evaluating for complications that may require immediate surgery. Baseline and serial abdominal x rays are then performed to follow the progression of colonic dilatation. 5. Patients should be admitted and evaluated, preferably in the intensive care unit, with frequent examinations to assess for signs of toxicity. 6. Both medical and surgical teams should co-manage patients on admission with daily evaluation.
[w128cab-35] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) 7. Female gender, age more than 40 years, hypoalbuminemia, acidosis, and high blood urea nitrogen levels are associated with high mortality in a previous study. This patient population requires a special attention from the admission. 8. It is important to examine the abdomen for hepatic dullness every day in patients who have severe colitis and are taking high-dose glucocorticoids because they might have a free perforation and not have classic signs of peritonitis. 9. The main goal for treatment for TM is to treat the underlying inflammation, restoring colonic motility, and preventing free colonic perforation.
[w128cab-36] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) 10. In HIV infection patients with TM, an aggressive search for infectious and noninfectious causes is essential, including early limited endoscopy and imaging studies. Patients with cytomegalovirus (CMV) colitis or C. difficile infection respond poorly to medical therapy and often require emergent subtotal colectomy and ileostomy. 11. Women with known risk factors for toxic megacolon (most commonly ulcerative colitis) should plan conception during a state of remission.
[w128cab-37] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=72, 2026-11-10) Conclusion TM is a condition best approached through the combined efforts of the medical and surgical team working with the patient. Medical therapy is the first-line treatment in most cases, but objective measures of improvement should be discussed before treatment initiation. If the patient manifests signs of hemorrhage, peritonitis, or perforation, surgery is emergently indicated. Subtotal/total colectomy and ileostomy is the procedure of choice in most instances, and a laparoscopic approach is potentially favored when possible and feasible.

Pseudomembranous colitis due to Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is a serious complication of antibiotic therapy and hospitalization that can progress to toxic megacolon, necessitating emergency colectomy. Patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) are particularly at high risk due to biliary obstruction, recurrent cholangitis, broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, and prolonged postoperative stays. We report the case of an elderly woman with multiple cardiovascular comorbidities and recent CDI who underwent PD for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). Prior to surgery, she had an endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with duodenobiliary (DB) stent placement due to obstructive jaundice, and biopsies confirmed PDAC. Intravenous cefotaxime was initiated because of signs of acute cholangitis. She subsequently developed CDI and completed treatment with oral vancomycin, which was discontinued only 11 days before the PD. Standard prophylaxis with cefazolin was administered prior to surgery. On postoperative day (POD) 1, empirical antibiotic therapy with cefotaxime was initiated. By POD 3, she developed profuse watery diarrhea, fever, and leukocytosis, with confirmed recurrent CDI. Despite prompt fidaxomicin therapy and intensive care, she rapidly deteriorated. Computed tomography (CT) revealed severe pancolitis and toxic megacolon. Emergency subtotal colectomy with end ileostomy was performed, and histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis. She eventually stabilized but remained frail, and the tumor board recommended best supportive care instead of adjuvant chemotherapy due to the disease stage and the severe course of CDI. This case underscores the need for early CDI testing in postoperative diarrhea following PD and for cautious perioperative antibiotic strategies in patients with recent CDI, as broad-spectrum antibiotics may precipitate fulminant recurrence.

It is characterized by profuse watery diarrhea, abdominal pain, fever, and marked leukocytosis, and may progress to toxic megacolon, perforation, septic shock, and death [ 1 ].
[w7853d3-3] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Well-established risk factors for CDI and pseudomembranous colitis include major abdominal surgery, exposure to healthcare facilities, use of proton pump inhibitors and H2 blockers, age, a history of CDI, a weakened immune system, malignancies and other systemic diseases, chemotherapeutic and immunosuppressive agents, and the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics. Patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) are particularly at high risk due to factors such as malignant biliary obstruction, preoperative biliary drainage, recurrent cholangitis, multiple courses of antibiotics, and frequent postoperative complications [ 2 , 3 ].
[w7853d3-4] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Despite this high-risk profile, pseudomembranous colitis following PD is rarely detailed in the surgical literature, and postoperative diarrhea is often initially attributed to pancreatic fistula, bile leak, delayed gastric emptying, intolerance to enteral nutrition, insufficient exocrine function of the pancreas, or sepsis from other causes [ 4 ]. Fulminant CDI with toxic megacolon requiring emergency colectomy in this context is exceptional [ 5 ].
[w7853d3-5] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) We report a case of fulminant pseudomembranous colitis requiring subtotal colectomy in an elderly patient after PD for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) of the pancreatic head. Additionally, we provide a brief review focusing on risk factors, diagnostic pitfalls, and surgical decision-making, with particular emphasis on perioperative antibiotic strategies in a patient with a recent history of CDI. The patient had provided written consent at admission for the use of anonymized data for scientific and research purposes. The manuscript was prepared in full anonymized form.
[w7853d3-6] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) CASE An 81-year-old woman in otherwise good biological condition, with a history of arterial hypertension, ischemic heart disease with sick sinus syndrome, and chronic venous insufficiency, was admitted to the internal medicine department in January 2024 with painless obstructive jaundice. An abdominal contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a 2-cm solid, heterogeneous mass in the uncinate process of the pancreas, with irregular dilatation of the main pancreatic duct (up to 13 mm) and marked dilatation of the common bile duct (up to 24 mm). There was no radiological evidence of vascular invasion, lymphadenopathy, or distant metastases ( Fig. 1 ). A chest contrast-enhanced CT scan also showed no lymphadenopathy, distant metastases, or any other lesions.
[w7853d3-7] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) The following day, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) was performed, during which a plastic duodenobiliary (DB) stent was placed to bridge the suprapapillary common bile duct stenosis, resulting in a subsequent decrease in bilirubin and cholestatic enzyme levels. Due to clinical and laboratory signs of cholangitis, intravenous cefotaxime was initiated at that time. Brush cytology and biopsy confirmed PDAC.
[w7853d3-8] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) During her first hospitalization, she developed antibiotic-associated diarrhea; stool testing was positive for Clostridioides difficile antigen and toxins A and B. She completed a full course of treatment with oral vancomycin, resulting in complete clinical resolution. Vancomycin was stopped 11 days before surgery. Therefore, at the time of PD, she had a recent history of hospitalization in a healthcare facility for C. difficile colitis related to third-generation cephalosporin exposure, pancreatic head malignancy, ERCP with biliary stenting, and multiple cardiovascular comorbidities.
[w7853d3-9] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) At the elective admission for PD, a preoperative physical examination found her to be a frail but hemodynamically stable woman, with no abdominal tenderness, no signs of ongoing infection, and no diarrhea. Laboratory tests showed mild anemia, normal renal function, and normalized inflammatory markers. After a multidisciplinary discussion, she was deemed fit for major surgery (American Society of Anesthesiologists class III).
[w7853d3-10] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Pancreaticoduodenectomy and early postoperative course The patient underwent a standard Whipple PD for PDAC of the pancreatic head. Intraoperatively, the tumor was located in the pancreatic head, involving peripancreatic tissue but without macroscopic distant metastases. Resection and reconstruction were completed without major technical difficulties, and no intraoperative complications occurred. Standard peripancreatic and abdominal drains were placed. Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis consisted of intravenous cefazolin according to institutional protocol.
[w7853d3-11] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Histopathological examination of the resection specimen revealed a poorly differentiated (high-grade) ductal adenocarcinoma (G3) of pancreatobiliary type, infiltrating peripancreatic fibroadipose tissue, the duodenal wall, and the common bile duct wall. The resection margins of the pancreas, duodenum, stomach, and bile duct were microscopically free of tumor; however, the tumor invaded the ventral resection margin, corresponding to an R1 resection according to current consensus criteria. One of 21 regional lymph nodes contained metastatic adenocarcinoma (pT2 pN1 M0, stage IIB).
[w7853d3-12] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) During the perioperative period, the patient remained hemodynamically stable and was extubated without respiratory distress. Postoperative drain output was serous, with no evidence of pancreatic fistula or bile leak. However, considering her age, frailty, comorbidities, and the risk of postoperative infectious complications following PD, she was closely monitored in the intensive care unit (ICU).
[w7853d3-13] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) On postoperative day (POD) 1, in the context of rising inflammatory markers (Leukocytes 33.1 × 10 9 /L) and concern for early infectious complications, empirical antibiotic therapy was initiated with intravenous cefotaxime at the discretion of the operating surgeon. At that time, cultures of intraoperatively collected bile had not yet been completed. The patient did not experience diarrhea.
[w7853d3-14] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Second episode of CDI and development of toxic megacolon During POD 2 (the day after cefotaxime escalation), the patient developed new-onset watery diarrhea. On POD 3, low-grade fever and increasing leukocytosis (25.7 × 10 9 /L) and C-reactive protein (CRP) (227.8 mg/L) were observed. Pancreatic fistula was ruled out due to a negative amylase level in the abdominal drain output. Intolerance to enteral feeding was not considered, as parenteral nutrition had been started early after the surgery. Empirical antibiotic therapy with oral fidaxomicin was initiated immediately due to concerns about a recurrence of Clostridioides difficile colitis. Stool testing again returned positive for C. difficile antigen and toxins A and B, indicating an early recurrent episode of CDI in the immediate postoperative period. The established antibiotic therapy with oral fidaxomicin was continued, along with bowel rest, meticulous fluid and electrolyte management, and close monitoring in the ICU.
[w7853d3-15] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) On POD 4, watery diarrhea persisted, and the patient developed abdominal distension with tenderness. The subhepatic drain adjacent to the hepaticojejunal anastomosis yielded cloudy serous fluid, which was collected for microbiological examination. Due to a continued rise in inflammatory markers, with CRP peaking at 328.4 mg/L and suspected concomitant intra-abdominal postoperative complications, intravenous meropenem was empirically added to the ongoing oral fidaxomicin therapy. Due to persistent abdominal distension, colonoscopic decompression was indicated on POD 5. The scope could only be safely advanced to 30 cm, revealing severe pseudomembranous colitis. The procedure was terminated due to the high risk of colonic perforation.
[w7853d3-16] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) On POD 7, microbiological culture from the abdominal drain was finalized, identifying vancomycin-resistant enterococcus. Intravenous tigecycline was added to the ongoing antibiotic regimen, which included oral fidaxomicin and intravenous meropenem. Despite these interventions, the patient’s clinical condition deteriorated on POD 9: abdominal distension worsened, bowel sounds diminished, and she developed signs of systemic inflammatory response and incipient septic shock.
[w7853d3-17] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) CRP was relatively low at 63.6 mg/L, however leucocyte count rose to 44.1 × 10 3 /µL, suggestive of an evolving septic state. A contrast-enhanced abdominal CT was performed, revealing marked circumferential thickening of the colonic wall with submucosal edema and mucosal enhancement, most pronounced in the splenic flexure, suggestive of pancolitis. The colon was significantly dilated, particularly the cecum and ascending colon, measuring approximately 11 × 6.5 cm, with no evidence of free intraperitoneal air ( Fig. 2 ). A moderate amount of free fluid was present in the peritoneal cavity, and there were no signs of mechanical obstruction. These findings were consistent with severe pseudomembranous colitis and toxic megacolon in the context of CDI. Given the rapidly progressive abdominal distension, systemic toxicity, and radiological evidence of toxic megacolon, the decision was made to proceed with emergency laparotomy and subtotal colectomy. This decision was reached in agreement with other specialists, despite the recent major pancreatic resection, as non-operative management was deemed unlikely to succeed, and delay could result in intestinal perforation and death.
[w7853d3-18] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Emergency colectomy and histopathology At laparotomy, the colon was massively dilated and edematous from the cecum to the sigmoid colon, exhibiting a thinned, friable wall and diffuse serosal hyperemia, but without visible perforation. A moderate amount of turbid peritoneal fluid was present, but there were no localized abscesses or anastomotic leaks. The small intestine appeared viable without significant dilation. A subtotal colectomy, including resection of the terminal ileum, was performed, along with the construction of a terminal ileostomy and closure of the rectal stump. Abdominal drains were placed in standard positions, and a right-sided chest drain was inserted due to pleural effusion.
[w7853d3-19] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Histopathological examination of the resected colon and terminal ileum revealed thickening of the entire intestinal wall with severe transmural inflammation, extensive mucosal ulcerationwith necrosis, and characteristic pseudomembrane formation consisting of adherent layers of inflammatory cells, bacteria, and fibrin exudate. These findings were consistent with severe pseudomembranous colitis and toxic megacolon. No cellular dysplasia or malignancy was identified, and regional lymph nodes showed only reactive changes.
[w7853d3-20] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Post-colectomy course and oncological outcome Following colectomy, the patient remained in the ICU, requiring vasopressor support and ongoing antibiotic therapy, which included intravenous tigecycline and vancomycin administered via rectal stump infusions. Over the subsequent days, the patient’s hemodynamic status gradually stabilized, inflammatory markers decreased, and lactate levels normalized. The ileostomy became functional with adequate output, and the patient tolerated a progressive reintroduction of enteral nutrition alongside pancreatic enzyme supplementation. No pancreatic leak or intra-abdominal abscess developed.
[w7853d3-21] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) The subsequent hospital course was complicated by transient pleural effusions that required chest drainage, a urinary tract infection caused by Candida albicans and Klebsiella pneumoniae , and an episode of somnolence and bradypsychia due to a mild traumatic brain injury. A brain CT scan performed for this episode revealed only chronic atrophic and ischemic changes without any acute pathology. Neurological and cardiological evaluations did not identify any new focal deficits or cardiovascular issues requiring intervention.
[w7853d3-22] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) With intensive physiotherapy and nutritional support, the patient gradually regained strength and mobility while using a walking frame and learned basic ileostomy care with assistance. A multidisciplinary tumor board reviewed the final histopathology, which revealed PDAC of the pancreatic head, classified as pT2 pN1 M0, poorly differentiated G3, with R1 resection. The board concluded that, under standard circumstances, adjuvant chemotherapy would have been indicated. However, considering the patient’s advanced age, comorbidities, and the severe postoperative course complicated by fulminant C. difficile colitis that required subtotal colectomy, the board recommended against initiating adjuvant chemotherapy. The patient was discharged to a rehabilitation facility for continued physiotherapy and stoma care education, with outpatient follow-up scheduled at the surgical and oncology clinics. Subsequent oncological follow-up focused on providing the best supportive care, with planned interval CT scans to reassess the need and feasibility of further systemic therapy.
[w7853d3-23] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Oncological follow-up and palliative chemotherapy During the follow-up in 2025, chest CT scans showed no evidence of metastatic disease. However, abdominal and pelvic CT scans revealed progressive retroperitoneal and intra-abdominal lymphadenopathy, consistent with a diagnosis of locoregional recurrence of pancreatic cancer. In April 2025 (15 months from diagnosis) a multidisciplinary gastrointestinal tumor board recommended palliative systemic therapy. The patient then underwent a total of four cycles of gemcitabine monotherapy, administered via an implanted venous port. An incidental bilateral internal jugular vein thrombosis was detected during port placement and was managed with temporary anticoagulation until ultrasound confirmed recanalization. The first cycle was complicated by grade 2 neutropenia, and after the fourth cycle, she developed decompensated type 2 diabetes mellitus, leading to a treatment interruption. At the most recent oncology visit, considering her multiple complications, borderline performance status (Karnofsky 60%–70%), and stable disease on imaging, a pause in systemic therapy with best supportive care and close surveillance was recommended.
[w7853d3-24] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) DISCUSSION CDI is a leading cause of healthcare-associated diarrhea and is strongly linked to recent antibiotic exposure and hospitalization. Postoperative CDI following major abdominal surgery increases morbidity, mortality, length of stay, and overall hospitalization costs, particularly in elderly, polymorbid, and frail patients. Patients undergoing PD are additionally burdened by malignant disease, biliary obstruction, preoperative biliary instrumentation, recurrent cholangitis, repeated courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics, malnutrition, and frequent postoperative complications, all of which further increase the risk of CDI and other infectious events [ 6 ].
[w7853d3-25] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Over the past three years, our department has identified a total of 50 cases of CDI, with 9 (4.5%) diagnosed in patients with hepatopancreatobiliary (HPB) conditions. Four cases occurred in patients who experienced complicated cholecystectomy with fistula formation or bile duct injury, two cases followed PD, and three followed total pancreatectomy. In two instances of pancreatic resection, patients had a history of prior biliary tract instrumentation and an indwelling DB stent. Overall, among 75 pancreatic operations, CDI developed in 5 patients, with two infections occurring after prior DB stent placement. Only one case, that of the patient presented here, followed a severe course, resulting in pseudomembranous colitis that required subtotal colectomy. In selected patients with preoperative biliary drainage or colonized biliary stents, broader antimicrobial prophylaxis—such as piperacillin–tazobactam—may be justified based on local microbiological patterns and institutional protocols to reduce postoperative infectious complications and surgical site infections [ 7 ]. However, particularly in patients at high risk for CDI, especially those with a recent history of CDI, the potential benefit of broader-spectrum prophylaxis must be carefully weighed against the increased risk of recurrent CDI. Any extension beyond standard perioperative prophylaxis should be strictly avoided, as it may precipitate CDI recurrence in susceptible patients.
[w7853d3-26] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Our patient presented several classic and procedure-specific risk factors: advanced age, PDAC, biliary stent, recent acute cholangitis treated with cefotaxime, proton pump inhibitor use, and a notable recent history of C. difficile colitis. The first episode of CDI was treated with oral vancomycin, with therapy completed only 11 days prior to the planned procedure. The patient received a prophylactic dose of cefazolin before surgery, and empirical antibiotic therapy with cefotaxime was initiated on POD 1. This constituted a second exposure to a third-generation cephalosporin within a short interval, which may have contributed to recurrence in a patient already carrying multiple established CDI risk factors. This sequence illustrates how repeated exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics in a high-risk patient following HPB surgery may lead to early onset, severe, or even fulminant CDI [ 8 ].
[w7853d3-27] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) CDI and pseudomembranous colitis after major abdominal HPB surgery Major abdominal surgery is associated with an increased risk of CDI due to the disruption of gut microbiota, perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis, prolonged fasting, and the frequent use of proton pump inhibitors. Postoperative CDI has been reported across various abdominal procedures and is consistently linked to increased morbidity, longer hospital stays, higher overall hospitalization costs, and elevated mortality rates. Fulminant CDI with toxic megacolon is a rare but life-threatening condition that often necessitates emergency colectomy [ 9 ].
[w7853d3-28] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) While CDI has been described following a wide range of abdominal surgical procedures, pancreatic surgery is considered a particularly high-risk setting [ 10 ]. In a large national cohort study involving over 76,000 patients undergoing hepatic and pancreatic resections, postoperative CDI occurred in approximately 1.1% of cases, with nearly three-quarters of infections arising after pancreatic resections. Notably, almost half of all CDI episodes developed within the first postoperative week and were associated with prolonged hospitalization and an increased risk of adverse outcomes, highlighting the early postoperative vulnerability of this population [ 11 ].
[w7853d3-29] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Recent evidence focusing specifically on PD further confirms the clinical significance of this complication. In a large national cohort study of patients undergoing PD for pancreatic cancer, postoperative CDI was linked to significantly worse perioperative outcomes, including increased morbidity and longer hospital stays. The study emphasized that CDI is an independent adverse prognostic factor in this surgical population, reinforcing its role as more than just a benign infectious complication [ 12 ].
[w7853d3-30] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Additionally, prior retrospective HPB studies have shown that pancreatic resection carries a disproportionate risk of infectious complications compared to other abdominal procedures, particularly when preoperative biliary drainage and biliary stents are involved. Bacteriobilia and multidrug-resistant biliary colonization have been identified as independent risk factors for postoperative infectious morbidity, including surgical site infections and clinically significant pancreatic fistula, reinforcing the idea that this patient population is particularly vulnerable to microbiota-related complications [ 6 , 7 , 13 ].
[w7853d3-31] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) However, despite the increasing evidence regarding postoperative CDI following pancreatic surgery, detailed case-level accounts of fulminant recurrent CDI progressing to toxic megacolon—necessitating emergency colectomy shortly after PD—are exceptionally rare. This case, therefore, provides clinically relevant insights by demonstrating how recent CDI, early postoperative exposure to systemic antibiotics, and complex HPB surgical conditions can interact to trigger a rapidly progressive and life-threatening course.
[w7853d3-32] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis and recurrent CDI risk Current guidelines for surgical antimicrobial prophylaxis classify PD as a gastroduodenal or upper gastrointestinal procedure. They recommend a single preoperative dose of a first-generation cephalosporin, such as cefazolin, as standard prophylaxis, with repeated administration only in prolonged operations or cases of major blood loss. Extending treatment beyond 24 hours or escalating to broader-spectrum antibiotics does not reduce the incidence of surgical site infections; instead, it is associated with an increased risk of CDI and the emergence of resistant organisms [ 14 , 15 ].
[w7853d3-33] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) For selected very high-risk patients who have recently experienced CDI and require systemic antibiotic therapy, some authors have proposed secondary prophylaxis with oral vancomycin during systemic antibiotic exposure to reduce recurrences. However, the evidence remains limited, and recommendations are not uniform [ 13 , 16 ]. Our case suggests that patients undergoing PD/HPB surgery who have recently experienced an episode of CDI may belong to this high-risk group.
[w7853d3-34] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Recurrent and fulminant Clostridioides difficile infection, Czech Guidelines Recurrent CDI refers to the return of diarrhea occurring within an eight-week period after the resolution of symptoms and the completion of therapy for a prior CDI episode. Diarrhea is defined as having at least three unformed stools within a 24-hour period [ 17 ]. According to the updated Czech Guidelines for the Treatment of CDI, the recommended therapy for a first episode in hospitalized patients is oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin. The first recurrence should be managed with either oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin, followed by fecal microbiota transplantation. In the case of a second or subsequent recurrence, oral vancomycin remains the treatment of choice and should again be followed by fecal microbiota transplantation. Fulminant CDI colitis should be treated with oral fidaxomicin or, when oral administration is not feasible, via alternative intraluminal routes, with the addition of intravenous tigecycline when indicated. In patients with sepsis, intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem should be used instead of tigecycline, in combination with fidaxomicin administered orally or via the same intraluminal routes. In cases refractory to medical management, a subtotal colectomy should be considered [ 18 ]. Antibiotic therapy followed the aforementioned updated Czech Guidelines for the treatment of CDI and was conducted in accordance with the recommendations of the local antibiotic center.
[w7853d3-35] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Diagnostic challenges after PD Early postoperative diarrhea after PD is often attributed to causes such as pancreatic exocrine insufficiency, enteral feeding intolerance, pancreatic fistula, biliary leak, or sepsis, particularly in patients already receiving broad-spectrum antibiotics. This can delay the consideration of CDI. In our case, the recurrent nature of CDI, its close temporal relationship to re-exposure to cefotaxime, and the rapid progression to systemic toxicity and toxic megacolon highlight the importance of maintaining a low threshold for CDI testing in any early postoperative diarrhea following PD. This is especially crucial for patients with a recent history of CDI.
[w7853d3-36] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) CT scans play a key role in differentiating between mechanical obstruction, anastomotic leak, diffuse colitis, and other complications. Typical findings in toxic megacolon include significant dilation and thickening of the colonic wall, accompanied by submucosal edema and mucosal enhancement, as observed in this patient. Although colonoscopy may reveal pseudomembranes, it is often unsafe for unstable patients with severe colitis and a risk of imminent perforation.
[w7853d3-37] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Indications for colectomy in fulminant CDI Guidelines for fulminant CDI recommend early surgical consultation and subtotal colectomy with end ileostomy for patients with toxic megacolon who have perforation or refractory shock despite maximal medical therapy. Delays in operative management are associated with higher mortality rates. Several studies suggest that performing colectomy before the onset of multiorgan failure results in better outcomes [ 5 ]. In our patient, the decision to proceed with colectomy was made when CT confirmed toxic megacolon and clinical signs indicated impending septic shock, despite ongoing targeted antibiotic therapy. This timely, proactive, and aggressive intervention likely contributed to her survival [ 19 ].
[w7853d3-38] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) This case underscores the challenging balance between the risks of re-operation in a patient who recently underwent PD and the high mortality associated with fulminant CDI if surgery is postponed. The favorable postoperative recovery after colectomy in this frail elderly woman supports the need for early and aggressive surgical management upon diagnosis of toxic megacolon.
[w7853d3-39] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Oncologic implications From an oncologic perspective, the patient’s final tumor stage (pT2 pN1, R1 resection, G3, stage IIB) typically indicates the need for adjuvant chemotherapy. However, the severe postoperative infectious course, the necessity for subtotal colectomy, and the patient’s subsequent frailty prompted the multidisciplinary tumor board to recommend best supportive care instead of adjuvant therapy. During follow-up, the patient developed radiologic evidence of locoregional recurrence, characterized by progressive retroperitoneal and intra-abdominal lymphadenopathy, while a chest CT scan showed no distant metastases. The patient received four cycles of palliative gemcitabine chemotherapy, which were complicated by grade 2 neutropenia, decompensation of type 2 diabetes, and bilateral internal jugular vein thrombosis. Following these complications, systemic therapy was paused in favor of best supportive care and imaging surveillance. According to Tummers et al. [ 20 ], the overall median survival after R1 resection for PDAC is 15 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 13–17), while patients with N1 disease, primarily corresponding to stage IIB, have a median overall survival of 14 months (95% CI, 12–16). In patients who received neoadjuvant treatment, Klaiber et al. [ 21 ] reported a median overall survival of 15.3 months after R1 resection. Teske et al. [ 22 ] found that in patients with N1 disease and R1 resection, the median overall survival was 13.6 months (95% CI, 10.7–18). Our patient survived 23.6 months after resection, despite a complicated postoperative course that required subtotal colectomy, which is notably longer than the reported median survival of 14–15 months for patients with N1 disease and R1 resection. This case illustrates that severe CDI and its surgical consequences may effectively preclude optimal oncologic therapy in high-risk pancreatic cancer patients. It also underscores the importance of rigorous CDI prevention and antibiotic stewardship strategies in this context, as these measures are crucial for reducing the risk of complications that could limit cancer treatment options.
[w7853d3-40] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) Practical implications for HPB surgeons Based on this case and available literature, several practical considerations may be relevant for HPB surgeons managing patients with recent CDI history. 1) Postoperative diarrhea after PD should prompt early CDI testing, particularly after recent antibiotic exposure. 2) Unnecessary prolongation or escalation of postoperative antibiotics should be avoided whenever possible. 3) Patients with preoperative biliary stents or recent cholangitis require careful balancing between infectious risk and CDI recurrence risk.
[w7853d3-41] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) 4) Early multidisciplinary collaboration involving surgeons, intensivists, infectious disease specialists and microbiologists is essential in rapidly deteriorating patients. 5) Early surgical consultation should be considered in fulminant CDI because delayed colectomy may worsen outcomes.
[w7853d3-42] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13, 2026-11-10) In conclusion, although CDI after major abdominal surgery is well recognized, fulminant recurrent CDI that progresses to toxic megacolon requiring emergency colectomy shortly after PD has been described in detail only rarely. In elderly, comorbid patients with recent CDI, biliary tract instrumentation, and repeated exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics, any early postoperative diarrhea should prompt testing for C. difficile and careful reassessment of antibiotic therapy. Perioperative antibiotic strategies should prioritize a single dose of narrow-spectrum, guideline-concordant prophylaxis and minimize prolonged use and postoperative exposure to broad-spectrum antibioticsto reduce the risk of recurrent, potentially fulminant CDI. Early recognition of toxic megacolon and timely subtotal colectomy can be life-saving, but may complicate or constrain subsequent oncological management, as altered bowel anatomy and stoma formation can worsen functional outcomes and tolerance of multimodal treatment. Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) generally occurs in individuals who have recently received antibiotics that alter the flora of the colon, allowing the C. difficile bacteria to flourish and produce toxins. The two main toxins produced by C. difficile are toxins A and B. These toxins damage the lining of the colon, causing inflammation and diarrhea. Individuals who are highly susceptible to CDI tend to be of advanced age, on antacids, or have inflammatory bowel disease [ 2 ]. However, community-acquired CDI is also known to occur in healthy individuals. Clostridioides difficile fulminant colitis is a severe infection characterized by the presence of hypotension, shock, or toxic megacolon. Fulminant C. difficile colitis is uncommon, with an incidence of 3% to 8% [ 3 ]. It is characterized by a mortality rate of 34% to 80% with surgical management vs. a 50% to 70% mortality rate in non-surgical care [ 4 ]. Standard treatment of fulminant C. difficile colitis includes aggressive antibiotic therapy with vancomycin and metronidazole, along with colectomy and ileostomy. In addition to the standard medical treatment for fulminant CDI, another procedure popularly known as the 'Pittsburgh protocol' focuses on colonic lavage after the creation of a loop ileostomy. The reported mortality in this set of patients was 19% [ 5 ].
[wef4de1-3] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=10, 2026-11-10) We present a rare case of severe small bowel CDI in a patient without any history of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or prior gastrointestinal (GI) surgeries. The patient was treated with small bowel resection, the creation of an ileostomy, antibiotic therapy, and the Pittsburgh protocol. This case illustrates that even though it may be rare, CDI can infect the small bowel and progress to the fulminant stage. This presents a challenge in the diagnosis as well as the surgical and medical management of these patients.
[wef4de1-4] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=10, 2026-11-10) Case presentation A 60-year-old female presented to the emergency department with non-bloody diarrhea, vomiting, poor appetite, abdominal cramps, and generalized weakness for four days. Past history was significant for IV drug abuse, neuropathy, deep vein thrombosis, anxiety, and depression. A month prior to the present admission, the patient was treated for pneumonia.
[wef4de1-5] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=10, 2026-11-10) At the time of presentation, the patient was stable. There were no signs of peritonitis. However, she deteriorated clinically over the next two to three days. Hypotension and tachycardia ensued. Leukocytosis and lactic acidosis worsened. The stool assay was positive for C. difficile . She was started on standard medical management with oral vancomycin and IV metronidazole. The CT scan revealed a small bowel ileus, but none of the colon was distended (Figure 1 ). Despite maximal medical management, her clinical condition did not improve. She underwent a diagnostic laparoscopy that revealed a necrotic small bowel (Figure 2 ) but a healthy-appearing colon. An exploratory laparotomy and resection of the small bowel were performed. The length of the small bowel resected was 70 cm. The length of the terminal ileum towards the ileocecal valve that was left behind was approximately 20.32 cm. The patient subsequently underwent a modified loop ileostomy. Catheters were placed proximally and distally in the bowel loops. One catheter was placed in the distal loop to provide polyethylene glycol and vancomycin to the distal bowel and colon. Another catheter was placed in the proximal small bowel to administer vancomycin to the proximal small bowel. The pathology report confirmed pseudomembranes in the resected small bowel. Clostridioides difficile was confirmed from ileostomy samples.
[wef4de1-6] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=10, 2026-11-10) Figure 1 Scout film from the abdomen/pelvis CT revealed distention of the stomach (horizontal arrow) and distended small bowel loops (vertical arrows) consistent with small bowel obstruction Figure 2 Intraoperative image taken during diagnostic laparoscopy showing necrotic small bowel (white arrow) The patient had ileus for a prolonged period, which made the administration of oral vancomycin difficult. In addition to the IV metronidazole treatment, vancomycin was administered through the long rubber catheter. Polyethylene glycol and vancomycin were flushed through the distal loop of the ileostomy daily until the WBC count normalized. The patient had a fecal management system in place to reduce source contamination. The patient's total hospital stay was about two months. She recovered well and was eventually transferred to a nursing home.
[wef4de1-7] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=10, 2026-11-10) Discussion It is important to note that C. difficile can infect more than just the colon. There have been 81 cases of small bowel CDI in the literature [ 1 ]. Small bowel CDI presents a unique challenge in many ways. Patients have ileus, making it difficult to institute oral vancomycin, which is the standard of care. Another challenge is the high mortality rate of approximately 30% [ 6 ].
[wef4de1-8] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=10, 2026-11-10) Patients with this unusual presentation typically have some type of predisposition, with colectomy being the greatest risk factor since it alters the small bowel flora by disturbing normal peristalsis and the function of the ileocecal valve [ 7 ]. Post-colectomy, the microbiome of the small intestine becomes similar to that of the colon, making it prone to a C. difficile infection, especially after antibiotic use [ 8 ]. Colonization of the small bowel can also occur because the mechanical action of the ileocecal valve is reduced or lost after surgery [ 9 ]. In our case, however, the patient had no prior colonic surgeries, making this case even more unique. We hypothesize that C. difficile retro-migrated from the colon to the small bowel across the ileocecal valve.
[wef4de1-9] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=10, 2026-11-10) The standard of care for fulminant CDI colitis includes high doses of oral vancomycin and IV metronidazole. In patients with paralytic ileus, vancomycin enemas can be beneficial since oral vancomycin may not reach the colon. Surgical management, which includes total colectomy with end ileostomy, is indicated for critically ill patients unresponsive to the antibiotic regimen. Despite standard medical care, including total colectomy with end ileostomy, mortality remains high [ 10 ].
[wef4de1-10] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=10, 2026-11-10) As demonstrated in our case, early diagnostic laparoscopy and/or laparotomy should be encouraged in patients who do not respond to medical management or clinically worsen despite treatment. For our patient, we used a joint approach in which the necrotic small bowel was resected, followed by the Pittsburgh protocol which consists of colonic lavage with warm polyethylene glycol through the loop ileostomy, followed by vancomycin flushes. Oral vancomycin was also continued for enteritis after the ileus resolved. Although the colon appeared normal during surgery, CDI of the colon affects the mucosal surface and should be treated until the patient improves clinically.
[wef4de1-11] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=10, 2026-11-10) Conclusions While small intestinal C. difficile remains uncommon, its occurrence could rise due to increasing C. difficile infections globally. Patients with small bowel CDI exhibit symptoms similar to those of C. difficile colitis. Colon appearance might be unaffected on imaging. If patients worsen or don't improve with medical treatment, diagnostic laparoscopy or laparotomy should be considered. Treatment lacks established guidelines due to its rarity, but our case suggests success by combining the Pittsburgh protocol, necrotic small bowel resection, and ongoing antibiotics. This approach could be considered for such cases.

[w63edc6-3] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id, 2026-11-10) UPDATED RECOMMENDATIONS Clostridioides difficile is a spore-forming anaerobic enteropathogen responsible for a wide spectrum of clinical features ranging from mild uncomplicated diarrhoea to severe debilitating disease, toxic megacolon, or even perforation and sometimes death. Risk factors for CDI include age >65 years, previous hospitalization and recent antibiotic therapy. Main virulence factors of C. difficile are toxins A (TcdA) and B (TcdB). The emergence and dissemination of a new hypervirulent strain (027/NAP/BI) in 2005 has stimulated clinical and basic research on C. difficile. Major advances have been made regarding the CDI epidemiology (better recognition of community acquired CDI), diagnosis (molecular tests) and therapy (new drugs such as fidaxomicin, bezlotoxumab, and fecal microbiota transplantation) aspects. These advances have allowed the updating of management recommendations, under the aegis of the
[w63edc6-4] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id, 2026-11-10) European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID). Antibiotic treatment of CDI depends on both the severity of the infection, and the risk/number of recurrences. Prevention of CDI requires an antimicrobial stewardship policy and the implementation of contact precautions for the infected patients.
[w63edc6-5] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id, 2026-11-10) Publisher: DES RECOMMANDATIONS ACTUALISÉES Clostridioides difficile est un bacille à Gram positif anaérobie sporulé responsable d’un large spectre d’infections digestives : de la diarrhée simple spontanément résolutive à la colite pseudomembraneuse qui peut se compliquer de mégacôlon toxique, de perforation et entraîner le décès du patient. Les principaux facteurs de risque d’infections à C. difficile (ICD) sont l’âge supérieur à 65 ans, l’administration d’antibiotiques et les antécédents d’hospitalisation. La virulence des souches est liée à la production de deux toxines, TcdA et TcdB. L’émergence et la dissémination rapide d’un clone dit « hypervirulent » (027/
[w63edc6-6] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id, 2026-11-10) NAP/BI) en 2005 a stimulé la recherche clinique et fondamentale. Des avancées majeures ont été réalisées tant sur le plan épidémiologique (meilleure reconnaissance des formes communautaires d’ICD), diagnostique (nouveaux tests moléculaires) que thérapeutique (nouveaux traitements comme la fidaxomicine, le bezlotoxumab, ou la transplantation de microbiote fécal) ; ces progrès ont permis la mise à jour des recommandations de prise à charge, sous l’égide de la
[w63edc6-7] (https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id, 2026-11-10) Société européenne de microbiologie clinique et des maladies infectieuses (ESCMID). Le traitement antibiotique des ICD dépend à la fois de la sévérité de l’infection, du risque et du nombre de récidives. Le contrôle de l’infection requiert, d’une part, la maîtrise de la consommation d’antibiotiques et, d’autre part, la mise en œuvre de précautions « contact » vis-à-vis des patients infectés.
