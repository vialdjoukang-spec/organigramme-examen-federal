## sgg-2
Constipation is defined as difficult, unsatisfactory or infrequent defecation. (Grade A) This definition is consistent with the definitions of chronic constipation used in recent guidelines and in the Rome IV consensus. Bowel Disorders. Gastroenterology. 2016; 150(6). Functional constipation refers to non-obstructive constipation, encompassing normal transit and slow-transit constipation. If not explained otherwise, constipation is equivalently used and refers to chronic constipation.

## sgg-4
Chronic constipation is a common disorder with a pooled global prevalence estimated at 14% (95% CI, 12–17%) in the general population and a rising prevalence with increasing age. Chronic constipation is a common medical condition affecting up to one in seven adults, in particular women and the elderly. The prevalence of chronic constipation is higher in women. (Grade A) The prevalence of chronic constipation increases with age. (Grade A) patients > 75 years compared to controls (OR 1.96, 95% CI 1.71-2.24). Premenopausal women (age < 49 years) have been shown to have longer transit times than older women (64.0 vs. 59.5 hours; difference 4.6 hours, 95% CI 1.1-8.1 hours). Constipation is a frequent medical condition, associated with a decrease in QOL. (Grade A)

## sgg-5
A positive family history of chronic constipation predisposes the individual to constipation. (Grade C) Studies with FC patients demonstrated a younger age at onset and longer duration of constipation when a positive family history of FC was present. This was associated with a higher incidence of complications (symptomatic hemorrhoids, anal fissures, rectal prolapse) and more frequent use of anal digitation.

## sgg-7
Lower social, economic and educational levels are associated with a higher prevalence of chronic constipation. (Grade C) the standardized prevalence rate (95% CI) for constipation symptoms was lowest in the 1st quintile (2.81 in males and 8.53 in females).

## sgg-8
In a review of medical records, 1,411 patients were analyzed between 1994 and 2011 by a single gastroenterologist. The majority of patients (68%) had normal transit constipation, 28% fulfilled the criteria for dyssynergic defecation and 4% suffered from slow-transit constipation as diagnosed by colon transit scintigraphy. Primary causes are classified into three categories: defecatory disorders, normal colonic transit and slow colonic transit constipation. Secondary causes of constipation are effects of other underlying diseases and include medications or anatomic alterations. After careful exclusion of a defecatory disorder with anorectal function testing including defecography, at least half of patients with functional constipation do not show signs of delayed colonic transit. (Grade C)

## sgg-9
Evacuation disorders represent an important underlying cause of chronic constipation and should be considered for treatment-resistant constipation before diagnosing isolated slow-transit constipation. (Grade B) one year after biofeedback therapy, only 20% of patients with STC maintained a beneficial effect compared to 50% of patients with pelvic floor dyssynergia.

## sgg-10
There is an increased prevalence of rectal hyposensitivity in chronic constipation. (Grade D) anorectal hyposensitivity in FC (27%) compared to constipation-predominant irritable bowel syndrome (IBS-C) patients (4%) using 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles for pain threshold of healthy volunteers (18 mmHg and 42 mmHg, respectively). Hypersensitivity was seen in 30% IBS-C patients and no FC patients. rectal hyposensitivity in patients with constipation (23%) and incontinence associated with constipation (27%) compared to patients with fecal incontinence only (10%) and patients without underlying constipation or fecal incontinence (5%).

## sgg-11
The volume of interstitial cells of Cajal in the sigmoid colon and the neuronal structures within the colonic circular smooth muscle layer are decreased in patients with slow-transit constipation. (Grade C) histological data come from selected patients with pronounced symptoms that may not be representative of ordinary chronic constipation.

## sgg-12
Lifestyle and dietary modifications must be the first-line therapeutic measures in the management of functional constipation. (Grade B) the majority of patients will respond to lifestyle modifications, improving their constipation symptoms, quality of life and general health. Further investigations should only be undertaken in patients who do not respond to this strategy.

## sgg-13
Increasing fiber is a widespread recommendation for functional constipation, including dietary fibers and fiber supplementation. (Grade C) a high-fiber diet alone does not appear to be effective without the combination of an adequate fluid intake.

## sgg-14
Additional fluid intake alone in patients who are not dehydrated does not have a positive effect on chronic constipation. (Grade C) Normal stools are composed of approximately 74% of water, while loose stools and hard stools are composed of >76% and <72% of water, respectively. A study including 833 elderly (mean age 74 years) reported that 71% of patients who were drinking at least 6 glasses of water per day had no difference.

## sgg-15
There is no consensus on the benefits and on the level of exercise required to improve chronic constipation. (Grade B) a minority of patients benefit from exercise. One review published in 2011 stands against the effect of exercise on constipation.

## sgg-16
Bulking agents, in particular soluble fiber, are recommended in the treatment of chronic constipation. (Grade B) A systematic review of 6 RCTs concluded that psyllium demonstrated a consistent efficacy over placebo with a number needed to treat (NNT) of 2. In 3 RCTs, psyllium improved global symptoms over placebo (86.5% vs. 47.4%). soluble fiber has an excellent safety profile and can be used during pregnancy and lactation.

## sgg-17
Gradual introduction of fiber to maximize efficacy and limit adverse effects is recommended. (Grade C) starting at 5 g/day (one teaspoon of psyllium) and raising the dose to 5 g twice or three times daily in weekly intervals. Adequate water intake (1.5 to 2 L/d) should be assured to maximize the effect.

## sgg-18
Osmotic laxatives, particularly polyethylene glycol (PEG), are recommended for patients with chronic constipation who fail or do not tolerate fiber. (Grade A) PEG 3350 or 4000, multiple products available in Switzerland, for example Movicol, with an NNT of 3 and a persistent effect for six months in the longest RCT and 24 months in a retrospective study.

## sgg-19
Lactulose is a treatment option in the treatment of patients with chronic constipation, limited by abdominal side effects. (Grade C) The pooled results demonstrate an NNT of 4. Common side effects include abdominal cramps and bloating and can lead to treatment discontinuation.

## sgg-20
There is insufficient data regarding the use of magnesium-based supplements in the treatment of chronic constipation. (Grade D) magnesium salts are widespread in Switzerland, there are no RCTs to support efficacy. The potential for side effects, especially in subjects with impaired renal function, should be taken into account.

## sgg-21
Bisacodyl is effective in the management of chronic constipation, at least as a short-term treatment. (Grade B) Sodium picosulfate is effective in the management of chronic constipation, at least as a short-term treatment. ## sgg-27
Prucalopride can be prescribed in refractory chronic constipation and stopped after 4 weeks, if no therapeutic benefit is seen. (Grade A) Linaclotide can be prescribed in refractory chronic constipation or IBS-C. (Grade A) Patients with refractory chronic constipation should be referred to centers with expertise in motility, excluding dyssynergic defecation, which is effectively treatable with biofeedback.

## sgg-29
Opioids constitute an essential component of advanced pain therapy and PAMORA are an effective remedy for opioid-induced constipation. (Grade A) registered for the Swiss market since 2015, the oral formulation was not officially distributed in Switzerland at the time of the statement. A fixed combination of naloxone with oxycodone is available as an alternative when an opiate is necessary.

## sgg-32
When constipation is caused by a functional defecation disorder, biofeedback is the treatment of choice based on the availability of local expertise. (Grade B) In Switzerland, there is a very good availability of specialized physiotherapists in all parts of the country. Biofeedback is effective in the long term and has no reported side effects. Patients with mere slow transit constipation do not seem to benefit from biofeedback, whereas slow transit combined with dyssynergia can normalize transit when defecation improves.

## sgg-35
Transanal irrigation has a place in the treatment of chronic constipation not adequately responsive to laxative therapy. (Grade C) efficacy of approximately 50%. Contraindications for any transanal procedure include active diverticulitis and fissures. The rate of perforation is under 3% and most common reason for patients to quit is embarrassment. Previous rectal surgery may increase the risk of perforation.

## sgg-37
Surgical treatment may be considered for selected patients if all other conservative treatments fail to improve the condition and quality of life. (Grade C) Surgery should only be offered after physiological tests and only if the cause lies within the colon or rectum. Colonoscopy, defecography and anorectal manometry in a highly specialized center are required before any operation, in order to exclude neoplasia. Perioperative complications occur in less than 20%. Either a Malone procedure or a loop ileostomy should be considered before resectional surgery.

## sgg-42
Surgery can be an effective treatment for an evacuation disorder due to structural causes (intussusception, rectocele, rectal prolapse, descending perineum syndrome) proven by imaging after failed conservative treatment. (Grade B) In Switzerland, laparoscopic ventral mesh rectopexy with a lightweight synthetic mesh remains a practiced procedure in skilled hands.

## sgg-alg
The algorithm starts from symptoms of difficult, unsatisfactory or infrequent defecation, then alarm symptoms and medications, history and physical exam including hints for evacuatory disorders, endoscopy, medication review, test for evacuatory disorders, lifestyle modifications with soluble fibre, osmotic laxatives as base therapy with stimulant laxatives on demand, then prokinetics and secretagogues, then referral to specialized centers.

## aips-66002
Chaque sachet de 25 ml contient 13,125 g de macrogol 3350, 178,6 mg de bicarbonate de sodium, 350,8 mg de chlorure de sodium et 50,2 mg de chlorure de potassium. Ce médicament contient 186,88 mg (8,129 mmol) de sodium par sachet. La posologie habituelle pour l'adulte est de 1 à 2 sachets par jour. Pour les patients âgés de plus de 65 ans, la prise d'un sachet par jour suffit généralement. Movicol Prêt à l'emploi ne doit pas être utilisé plus de 3 mois sans prescription médicale. La dose supérieure utilisée en cas de coprostase peut être utilisée au plus pendant 3 jours. Les patients emmènent un litre de liquide par jour. Le traitement de la coprostase comprend 8 sachets par jour, pris en l'espace de 6 heures. L'utilisation n'est pas recommandée chez l'enfant de moins de 12 ans. Contre-indications : hypersensibilité, obstruction ou perforation intestinale, iléus, maladies inflammatoires graves de l'intestin comme la maladie de Crohn, la colite ulcéreuse ou le mégacôlon toxique. Si la fonction cardio-vasculaire est altérée, pas plus de 2 sachets en une heure. Il est impératif de cesser immédiatement l'administration en cas de variation de l'équilibre hydro-électrolytique. Les autres médicaments ne doivent pas être pris par voie orale une heure avant, pendant et une heure après. Numéro d'autorisation 66002 (Swissmedic). Présentation : 20 sachets. Mise à jour Novembre 2022. Norgine AG. Une diarrhée après une quantité supérieure conduit à arrêter jusqu'à ce que la diarrhée cesse, puis à reprendre plus bas.
