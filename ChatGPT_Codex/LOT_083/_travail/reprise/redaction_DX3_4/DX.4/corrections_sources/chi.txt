1.0 GUIDELINE STATEMENT:

Neurovascular assessment skills are an integral part of acute patient care. They are essential for
early recognition of neurovascular compromise which may lead to permanent deficits, loss of
limb or even death (Schreiber, 2016). The purpose of this guideline is to provide an educational
and evidence based practice resource for nursing staff involved in neurovascular assessment of
paediatric patients. It outlines the required assessment process to promptly recognise clinical
change. It supports decision making when interpreting assessment findings and implementing
appropriate interventions.


This guideline should be used in conjunction with:
       Neurovascular Assessment Upper Limb Observation Chart (see Appendix 1).
       Neurovascular Assessment Lower Limb Observation Chart (see Appendix 2)
        Post Spinal Surgery Neurovascular Observations Upper and Lower Limbs (see Appendix
        3)


2.0 PURPOSE:

This guideline aims to:

       Ensure consistent, accurate neurovascular assessments in paediatric patients.
       Support early identification of neurovascular compromise.
       Guide appropriate and timely escalation of care.
       Provide a structured educational resource for nursing staff.
       Enhance      communication between multidisciplinary teams through                          consistent
        documentation.
       This procedure should be used in conjunction with CHI's Upper and/or Lower Limb
        Neurovascular Observation Charts (Appendices 1 & 2).

3.0 SCOPE:

This guideline applies to all nursing staff involved in neurovascular assessment.




4.0 DEFINITIONS/ABBREVIATIONS:

       Neurovascular Assessment – Evaluation of sensory, motor, and circulatory function in
        limbs
       Compartment Syndrome - A surgical emergency caused by increased pressure within a
        closed muscle compartment, compromising blood flow and tissue perfusion.
       Capillary Refill Time (CRT) - Time taken for colour to return to an external capillary bed
        after pressure is applied; normal is <2–3 seconds.
       Tingling/Paresthesia - A sign of nerve involvement, often indicating early neurovascular
        compromise.
       Doppler Scan - A manual device used to detect weak or absent pulses when not palpable


5.0 RESPONSIBILITIES:

It is the responsibility of:
    5.1 Registered Nurses (RNs) - Conduct, document, and interpret neurovascular assessments
    and escalate concerns
    5.2 Clinical Nurse Managers (CNMs) - Ensure staff compliance and competency with
        neurovascular assessment
    5.3 Medical/Surgical Teams - Respond to reported neurovascular concerns and implement
        necessary interventions
    5.4 Educators/Practice Development Coordinators - Provide training and updates on
        neurovascular assessment procedures.
    5.5 Parents/Carers - Receive education on signs of neurovascular compromise, especially at
        discharge.

6.1 Assessment Overview Upper and Lower Limbs:

Neurovascular assessment must include:

       Skin Colour and Temperature
       Sensation and Motor Function
       Oedema
       Peripheral Pulses
       Capillary Refill Time (CRT)
       Pain Assessment
Compare affected and unaffected limbs. Subtle differences may indicate early compromise and
should be reported immediately.

6.1.1 Skin Colour and Temperature

       Use dorsum of hand to assess temperature.
       Pallor or coolness may suggest arterial insufficiency.
       Warmth with cyanosis may suggest venous congestion.
       Consider skin tone variations when assessing cyanosis (Sommers, 2011).
       Shiny, pale, tight skin may indicate increased pressure.

 6.1.3 Oedema

        Monitor for increasing swelling, which may impair circulation and require escalation.

        Check bilaterally and at the most distal accessible points.
        Assess if pulses are strong, weak or absent
        Use Doppler if pulses are not palpable.
        Mark pulse points for consistency in reassessments.

       Compress nail bed for 5 seconds; normal refill ≤ 2–3 seconds.

       Disproportionate pain, unrelieved by analgesia, especially on passive movement, may

       In non-verbal patients, assess for behavioural signs (restlessness, grimacing, tachycardia,
        etc.).

6.1.7 Observation Frequency

       Hourly for first 24 hours post-injury/surgery/application of cast.
       Then 4-hourly for 48 hours or as clinically indicated.
       Increase frequency if deviations from baseline occur.
       Cardiac catheterisation patients:
            o    Include neurovascular observations with post-anaesthetic sets.
            o    Monitor puncture sites with each observation.


6.1.8 Documentation

       Baseline neurovascular assessment on admission.
       Use appropriate Upper or Lower Limb Observation Chart.
       Document all deviations and notify the medical/surgical team.
       Note any nerve block or other influencing factors.


6.1.9 Management of Neurovascular Compromise

       Elevate limb, no higher than heart level.
       Notify treating team
       Split plaster casts or cut/remove bandage as advised
       Maintain limb alignment.
        If neurovascular status improves keep affected limb elevated and continue to monitor
        closely.
        If neurovascular status does not improve or continues to deteriorate, the patient may
        need to attend theatre for pressure monitoring and/or fasciotomy.


        For cardiac catheter patients:
        If any changes to neurovascular observations (i.e. decrease in pulse pressure, change in

6.2 Potential Complications

6.2.1 Compartment Syndrome

        Compartment Syndrome is a serious complication of musculoskeletal injury. Compartment
        Syndrome results from an increase in pressure inside a compartment, which comprises of
        muscles and nerves and is enclosed by fascia. Fascia is inelastic and does not expand to
        increased volume or pressure. When the compartment pressure increases, nerves and then
        muscles become compressed resulting in decreased blood flow and tissue perfusion,
        muscle ischemia and loss or altered sensation. Compartment Syndrome is a surgical
        emergency to relieve the pressure or reduce volume within the compartment, which will
        preserve blood supply, tissue perfusion and function. Early recognition of neurovascular
        deterioration is crucial in limb salvage or survival.
        The medical team should be contacted immediately as soon as compartment syndrome is
        suspected. Early treatment includes removal of constricting bandages or cast. The limb
        should be laid flat or slightly raised to a maximum of 10% above the patient’s heart. Higher
        levels are not recommended as the arteriovenous pressure difference decreases, which can
        further impair the circulation to the affected area. Surgical fasciotomy is considered the
        most effective treatment (Jerome, 2020; Lin & Samora, 2020; Mortensen et al., 2020).


6.2.2 Indications of compartment syndrome include:

        Pain: The first and most reliable sign of compartment syndrome. Pain out of proportion
         to injury, extreme pain on passive movement and pain unrelieved with opioid analgesia.
        Paralysis: Is generally a late sign of compartment syndrome and results from prolonged
         nerve compression or muscle damage. Paralysis presents with inability to actively move
         the limb and increased pain on passive movement that is not relieved in extension.

6.3 Spinal Neurovascular Observations

6.3.1 Purpose and Importance
      Spinal neurovascular assessment following spinal surgery is essential to:
        Detect early signs of spinal cord compromise, nerve injury, or vascular insufficiency.
        Prevent irreversible neurological deficits through timely intervention.
        Ensure safe recovery and maintain optimal function of the lower limbs.



6.3.2 Observation Protocol

        Initial Post-Operative Phase:
        Perform spinal neurovascular observations as per standard post-op monitoring (e.g.,
        immediately post-op, then at 15 min for one hour, 30 min for one hour, 1 hourly
        thereafter).
        Ongoing Monitoring:
        Continue spinal neurovascular observations every 2 hours overnight or as directed by
        the consultant.

6.3.3 Components and Rationale
      1. Motor Function (Lower Limb Movement)
            a. What to check: Ability to move toes, ankles, knees.
            b. Rationale: Weakness or paralysis may indicate spinal cord compression or nerve
                 root injury.
      2. Motor Function (Upper limb Movement)
            a. What to check: Ability to move fingers, wrists and elbows.
            b. Rationale: Weakness or paralysis may indicate spinal cord compression or nerve
                 root injury.
      3. Sensory Function (Light Touch, Pain, Temperature)
             a. What to check: Compare sensation bilaterally using light touch and pinprick.
             b. Rationale: Loss or alteration of sensation can signal nerve compromise or
               ischemia.
      4. Circulatory Status
             a. What to check: Capillary refill, limb temperature, colour, and distal pulses.
             b. Rationale: Poor perfusion may indicate vascular compromise or compartment
            syndrome.
      5. Pain Assessment
            a. How to check: Using age-appropriate pain assessment tool on PEWS
            b. What to check: New or worsening pain, especially disproportionate to expected
                 post-op pain.
            c. Rationale: Severe pain can be an early sign of neurovascular compromise or
                 hematoma.

6.4 Discharge and Parent Information

      For patients at risk of neurovascular compromise, education on neurovascular assessment
      is crucial. Age appropriate education should be provided to the patient, including
      encouragement for the patient to move their digits regularly.


      Educate parents on the importance of performing neurovascular assessment and why it is
      necessary to disturb the patient when sleeping while in hospital.


      Many patients who are at risk of neurovascular compromise, leave hospital before the
      risk of compartment syndrome is over. Parents should be educated regarding the signs
      and symptoms of neurovascular compromise and when to return to their GP or
      discharging hospital if they are concerned.
